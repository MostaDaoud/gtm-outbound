# Research

Finding out what is true about your market before you write claims about it.
Every claim carries its lesson and timestamp.

The corpus is unusually consistent on one point: what customers *say* is weak
evidence. Two independent distortions are documented — confabulated reasons and
politeness — and both are covered below.

---

## 1. Why stated preference is unreliable

**Distortion one: people invent their reasons.** Buyers rationalise after
deciding, so purchase-reason answers are alibis rather than causes. Schutz's
instruction is blunt: do not believe customers who tell you why they bought
(Schutz, *1.2 Characteristics System 2* @ 32:50). Full mechanism in
`psychology.md` §1.

*Buyology* reaches the same practical warning—what people say and what they do
can differ—while arguing for brain-based research (Martin Lindstrom,
*Buyology - Part 01* @ 05:44). Treat that as support for triangulating
self-report with behaviour, not as blanket validation of every neuromarketing
claim in the book. The source itself uses sweeping language about neuroimaging;
LinkedIn Lab preserves the attribution instead of upgrading it into established
fact.

**Distortion two: people are polite.** The courtesy bias — "people tend to be
socially correct rather than truthful" — skews satisfaction research positive
unless the respondent is genuinely angry, "in which case it may work in the other
direction" (*Digital Psychology & Persuasion, 18-Cognitive Biases >
04-Courtesy Bias* @ 00:02).

Two mitigations from that lesson (@ 00:43):

- **Ask questions that invite nuance** rather than ones that elicit "I was really
  happy with everything." Dig for what could have been better.
- **Do not confuse feedback about a person with feedback about the experience.**
  A warm review of a support rep is not evidence the process worked.

**What follows:** weight behaviour above stated preference. Where you must ask,
ask about past specific episodes rather than general feelings or predicted
future behaviour.

### Jobs-to-be-done interview: reconstruct the switch

The new Jobs-to-be-Done lesson supplies the concrete interview sequence that
the earlier research material was missing. Do not ask whether someone *would*
use a proposed feature; people are poor at predicting their own behaviour.
Reconstruct the past instead (*Product Marketing, 13-Radical Differentiation >
05-everyone-hates-marketers__best-of-how-to-use-jobs-t* @ 33:50):

1. What did life look like before this product?
2. What was good and bad about the old way?
3. What happened that finally made you look for something else?
4. How did you search—Google, friends, or an option already known?
5. What almost stopped you from buying? (@ 34:28–35:02; @ 39:35)

The lesson's useful metaphor is to "film a documentary" of the events leading
to purchase, not collect an opinion about the product. Start small: two to five
recorded, transcribed interviews can uncover material the team did not know
internally (@ 36:53).

### Two questions for value-proposition evidence

For paying customers, ask open-endedly for (1) the top benefit or best result
they received and (2) why they chose this option over the alternatives. The
first identifies desired outcome; the second identifies the competitive edge
that tipped the decision (*13-Radical Differentiation >
11-everyone-hates-marketers__value-proposition-what-is* @ 10:37–11:56).

Keep timing honest: give customers enough time to experience the product before
asking about realised benefit. A separate immediate post-purchase question can
capture why they chose it (@ 15:10).

### Keep judgments independent before discussion

Kahneman's halo-effect correction is directly useful for copy review and
research synthesis. He stopped grading one student's essays in sequence and
instead scored every answer to question one before moving to question two; the
less coherent result felt less comfortable but was more faithful to the
evidence (Daniel Kahneman — *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, @ 03:58:30–04:02:00).

The general rule is to **decorrelate error**: collect individual judgments before
reviewers influence one another. Aggregation reduces error only while the errors
remain sufficiently independent; shared discussion can collapse several
observations into one repeated bias (@ 04:02:00–04:04:00). For a copy review,
have reviewers score or annotate independently, then compare. For research,
record each source before the team converges on a narrative.

## 2. Segmentation

**Five types**, taught as behavioral, geographic, and three others, each with its
own attributes (*Product Marketing, 07-Segmentation and Persona Research >
03-Understanding Segmentation* @ 16:53).

**A five-step build process**, deliberately assuming no special tooling — "assuming
that technology is not in the mix" (*07-Segmentation and Persona Research >
05-How to Build Segments* @ 01:22). It opens with preliminary research to
establish "what are some of the attributes and things that you should be fielding
for when you design your survey."

That ordering is the lesson. Preliminary qualitative work decides what the survey
asks; a survey designed before it measures your assumptions back at you.

**Cross-reference worth holding.** Schutz argues contextual and situational data
outperforms personality-style segmentation, and warns that personality models are
built on questionnaires asking people how they *think* they would respond — "in
terms of methodology you're in a worse nightmare" (*6.1 Self & Situation* @ 18:30).
He is not disputing segmentation; he is disputing segmenting on self-reported
traits. Segment on what people did and the situation they were in.

### Worldview is observed in stories, not declared in a profile

Seth Godin's added interview makes the same point from a cultural angle. People
will not reliably explain their worldview, but "give it all away" through the
stories they tell and how they behave in the environment
(*13-Radical Differentiation >
12-everyone-hates-marketers__seth-godin-s-marketing-se* @ 07:43). Observe first,
form a hypothesis about why the system evolved that way, then test a bounded
change rather than redesigning everything at once (@ 10:56–12:19).

Do not treat one campaign as capable of changing a worldview. The interview's
constraint is to meet people who are ready for the intervention where they are;
worldviews move through long-term culture, not one marketing message
(@ 34:47–35:36). This puts an evidence boundary around persona work: select the
smallest viable audience whose worldview already makes the promised change
plausible (@ 18:17–18:44).

## 3. Content and theme research

Treat it as qualitative coding, not brainstorming
(*01-Content Marketing Research > 05-Identifying themes and topics* @ 00:16):

1. **Code the data** — break it into individual themes. "Very similar to how you
   would code data from a qualitative survey."
2. **Distil the coded list** further.
3. **Map themes onto topics** for the site.

The stated goal is to "take that huge pile of data that we collected in lesson
three and start to distill that into some good themes" (@ 00:00) — note that
collection is a *prior* step. Coding operates on evidence already gathered, not
on opinion.

## 4. Competitive intelligence

**Tie it to a business metric or it does not count.** Deliverables like battle
cards "are great deliverables that you should plan on producing. But the goal
should drive back to a business critical metric like revenue or win rate"
(*11-Competitive Intel  Market Research > 11-Pushing Go On Your Competitive
Program* @ 02:49).

**Consistency is the operational requirement**, with specific cadences from the
same passage:

- Update battle cards every **45–60 days**
- Do not skip a month with your newsletter
- Do not let **six months** pass without speaking to a closed-lost or closed-won
  account

The closed-lost conversation is the highest-value item on that list and the
easiest to defer. It is also the one research channel less exposed to courtesy
bias (§1) — people who did not buy have less social reason to flatter you.

### Analyst relations, in one line

The corpus carries a full **15-Analyst Relations** module — AR's role in product
marketing, evaluating firms and analysts, relationship maps, building influence,
leveraging AR deliverables, benchmarking a programme and elevating it to tier 1.

It is the furthest module from copywriting in the library, and it is listed here
so that a search for it returns something rather than silence. Two things in it
generalise: analysts are a **third-party source of category language** — useful
input when you are establishing a new concept per `positioning.md` §4 — and the
programme is measured the same way competitive intel is, against influence on
revenue rather than deliverables produced.

Go to the module itself for anything beyond that; nothing in it has been
distilled here.

## 5. Measuring what research and enablement actually did

Honest framing from the enablement module: "it can be very difficult to isolate
the specific revenue impact of one particular enablement program... so many
things are changing in the context of a business from product configurations,
product offerings, pricing" (*10-Sales and Customer Success Enablement >
02-Measuring Enablement* @ 01:47).

The recommended response is not to fake attribution but to **report in the
funnel shape the company already uses**, so results are legible next to sales and
demand-gen numbers and the conversation is about "what's happening, what's
working, and where are there areas to improve" (@ 01:47).

Applies directly to copy work. Claiming a page rewrite caused a revenue number is
usually indefensible; showing movement at a funnel stage, in the company's own
reporting frame, is both honest and more persuasive internally.

## 6. Working sequence

1. **Gather behaviour first** — analytics, closed-lost calls, support logs,
   recordings. Cheap and undistorted.
2. **Reconstruct the switch.** Ask for the sequence before, trigger, search,
   choice, and almost-stopped objection. (§1)
3. **Qualitative before quantitative.** Preliminary research decides what the
   survey asks. (§2)
4. **Design questions against the courtesy bias.** Specific past episodes, room
   for negatives. (§1)
5. **Code and distil into themes.** (§3)
6. **Collect judgments independently before discussion.** (§1)
7. **Segment on situation, behaviour, and observed worldview**, not
   self-reported personality. (§2)
8. **Put a cadence on competitive work** or it will lapse. (§4)
9. **Report in the company's existing funnel frame.** (§5)

## 7. New-source additions: build an evidence chain

### Claim → reason → evidence → warrant

*The Craft of Research* separates a claim from the reason offered for it, the
evidence supporting that reason, and the warrant that explains why the evidence
is relevant. The warrant can stay implicit for experts, but copy aimed at a
mixed audience should make the missing bridge visible. The authors also advise
checking an important quotation or statistic against the primary source instead
of relying on a secondary summary. (*The Craft of Research, Fourth Edition*,
chapters 6 and 11.)

Use this as a proof audit: **What exactly is the claim? What observation supports
it? What makes that observation relevant? What would a skeptical reader object
to?**

### Data describes; models and tests explain

Pearl and Mackenzie distinguish predictive summaries from causal explanations:
data alone cannot answer “why” without a model of the process that generated the
data. Bergstrom and West add practical checks for selection bias, misleading
axes, overfitting, and null models that reproduce a pattern without the claimed
cause. (*The Book of Why*, pages 13 and 316; *Calling Bullshit*, chapters 6,
7, 8, and 11.)

**linkedin-lab application:** label every statistic as descriptive, correlational,
or causal; preserve the original population and timeframe; and never turn a
correlation or a null-model-sensitive pattern into a causal promise.

### Interview for episodes, not compliments

Rob Fitzpatrick's Mom Test directs interviews toward specific past episodes:
when did the problem last happen, how was it handled, what else was tried, and
what did it cost? Pre-plan the three most important things to learn for each
audience type, then deflect compliments and future hypotheticals back to
observable behaviour. (*The Mom Test*, chapters Two and Three; “Prepare your
list of 3.”)

### Separate decision quality from outcome

Annie Duke's “bet to learn” framing is a useful test log for copy experiments:
an outcome can be good or bad for reasons unrelated to decision quality. Record
the hypothesis, confidence, expected alternatives, and what the result actually
updates before declaring a winner. (*Thinking in Bets*, chapters One, Three,
and Five.)
