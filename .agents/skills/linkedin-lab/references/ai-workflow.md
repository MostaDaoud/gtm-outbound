# AI Workflow

Using AI tools on copy work without producing what everyone else produces. Every
claim carries its lesson and timestamp.

---

## 1. The commodity problem

The premise the whole reference rests on:

> "anyone who has an AI tool, anyone who has $20 for ChatGPT Plus can now produce
> the same article as you very quickly and very cheaply. **That advantage is now
> gone**... If you want to create truly helpful content, you need something
> unique. You need to be bringing something new to the table."
> (Kevin Meng, *02-The Fundamental Principles > 07-Principles* @ 01:14)

The questions he offers, attributed on the recording to Kyle Roof at Page
Optimizer Pro: "What's something new you're bringing to the table? What's a new
angle? What's a new piece of **personal experience**? What's a new piece of added
value?" (@ 01:14).

**Personal experience is the one AI structurally cannot supply.** It is also the
one most easily cut for time. Treat it as the required ingredient, not the
garnish.

---

## 2. You still have to know the job

Meng's framing, and the most useful sentence in the corpus about AI copy:

> "even with this amazing autopilot you still need somebody who knows how to fly
> the damn plane... ChatGPT can produce the words for you, listen to instructions,
> outline, research and even self-[edit]. However, you still need to know how to
> write content if you want to produce good content."
> (*01-Introduction > 01-PostHello* @ 02:20)

What he says the operator supplies (@ 02:20):

- Tell it **what to write**
- Tell it **what your audience wants**
- Tell it **what to sound like**
- Tell it **what to edit**
- Make **high-level edits by hand** for engagement and conversion

"You need to be the one that explains to ChatGPT what good is." That is the whole
skill requirement: you cannot specify quality you could not recognise.

## 3. Conversation, not prompt

Bill Mueller's account of how the good output actually arrives: "what's
interesting about the process that you put this together is **the back and
forth**... sometimes I'll put a prompt in — I do mine with voice messages and
explain my thing — and then I get something really good back. But usually..."
(*Drop Dead Copy, 01-Bill Mueller AI Copy Secrets Vol 1* @ 23:29).

Two practices worth lifting:

- **Iterate rather than perfect the first prompt.** The single-shot mega-prompt is
  the less effective pattern in the room.
- **Dictate.** Speaking the brief captures nuance and context that typing tends
  to compress away.

His reaction to a good output is also diagnostic — "it makes me want to go look
it up" (@ 23:29). Curiosity in the *writer* is a decent proxy for curiosity in
the reader. See `headlines.md` §1.

### The voice-of-customer upgrade pass

Eden Bidani's method is the corpus's most concrete answer to "how do I beat the
tool rather than compete with it." It takes two inputs — **AI-generated copy**
and **voice of customer**, sourced online if you have none of your own — and runs
a landing page headline through "two or three steps depending on how far you want
to push it" (*Product Marketing, 12-Positioning > 04-Write better than AI*
@ 03:02, @ 04:01).

Her framing of the goal: "How do you level up AI-generated copy? How can you do
it even better than the AI tools already do?" The process generalises — "whether
it's an ad, a landing page, a product page, you name it" — and the speed comes
from having both inputs to hand before you start (@ 03:32).

**The constraint that makes it worth knowing:** it is explicitly designed to be
safe where you *cannot* use a trained custom GPT — including when the client
will not permit one (@ 04:01). Most of the advice in this file assumes you can
build persistent context. This is the fallback when you cannot.

The through-line with §1: the differentiator is the customer's own language,
which the model does not have and you can go and get.

## 4. Use AI to train yourself, not only to produce

The most transferable prompt in the corpus. Shiv Shetti, verbatim from the
lesson, "you can use this pretty much verbatim"
(*08-Shiv Shetti AI Copy Secrets Vol 1* @ 09:41):

> "I'm a freelance copywriter who wants to practice my skills and develop
> world-class [work]. To help with this, I want you to create a fake client brief
> for me to read through and practice writing one email for, which I want you to
> give me detailed feedback on with a grade for each part of the email. To start,
> I want you to create a particularly challenging brief that tests my ability to
> understand market saturation, the five levels of awareness, optimal product
> positioning, and more."

Three things make it work, and they generalise to any practice prompt:

1. **It asks for the problem, not the solution** — a brief to write against.
2. **It requests graded, per-section feedback** rather than a verdict.
3. **It specifies the difficulty axis** — market saturation, awareness levels,
   positioning — so the challenge lands where you want to improve.

This inverts the usual relationship: the AI is the client and the examiner, you
are the writer. It builds the judgement §2 says you need.

## 5. Where AI genuinely scales

Sequence production. "The whole purpose of this was to show you how you could use
this to create an entire sequence — a seven email welcome sequence, a five email
promotional launch sequence, a 20 email customer onboarding sequence. It's really
limited by what you can imagine and what you can communicate"
(*03-John McIntyre AI Copy Secrets Vol 1* @ 49:07).

Note the constraint he names: imagination and communication, not model
capability. If your sequences come out generic, the brief was generic.

Pair with `emails.md` §1 — supply the per-email **objective** yourself, and let
the tool draft against it.

| Research summarisation | Final line edits for engagement (§2) |
| Practice briefs and critique (§4) | Judgement about what good is (§2) |

## 7. AI-assisted offer ideation: draft, test, verify

Ben Preston's *100M Offers Made Easy* is a derivative source that shows how to
turn Hormozi's four-part value equation into a structured AI prompt. The useful
pattern is procedural: ask the model to inspect dream outcome, perceived
likelihood, time delay, and effort/sacrifice; request concrete improvements; then
compare alternatives and test them (Ben Preston — *100M Offers Made Easy*,
chapters “Offer Ideation” and “Testing Your Offers for Irresistibility”).

Use that pattern in four passes:

1. Ask for questions and missing evidence before asking for polished copy.
2. Have the model score or compare variants as a heuristic, never as a
   conversion forecast.
3. Replace generic suggestions with the customer's proof, constraints, and
   voice.
4. Test the resulting offer or message with real behaviour and feed the result
   back into the next iteration.

Do not ask the model to impersonate a living writer or treat a prompt-generated
“offer score” as research. The derivative book is a workflow aid; the primary
offer principles and the client's evidence remain the authority.

## 8. Checklist

1. **What is in this that could not have come from a model?** If nothing, it is
   commodity. (§1)
2. **Did you tell it what good sounds like, or hope it knew?** (§2)
3. **Did you iterate, or accept the first output?** (§3)
4. **Did you supply the objective, or let it invent one?** (§5)
5. **Are you using it to get better, or only to get finished?** (§4)
6. **Did a human make the last pass?** (§2)
