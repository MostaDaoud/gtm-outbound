# Emails

Sequences, single sends, and the offer mechanics that go in them. Every claim
carries its lesson and timestamp.

---

## 1. AIDA is the email default

Medhora treats AIDA as the working structure for email specifically — "it's a
very popular formula and it's super interesting, **especially for emails**"
(*02-Copy Concepts > 02-The AIDA Model What It Is and How to Use It* @ 00:00).

**His actual method is mechanical.** Write the four words down the page, then
fill them in: "I literally write out attention, interest, desire, action"
(*01-Copywriting Course > 05-BONUS VIDEO* @ 33:47). Not a lens applied
afterwards — a scaffold you type first.

**The unit of planning is subject line + objective + body.** He shows a student's
full autoresponder sequence laid out exactly that way: "subject line, objective,
email copy — look: attention, interest, desire" (*01-Videos > 244-Follow this
AIDA formula for everything you write* @ 05:44).

Naming an **objective** per email is the underrated column. It forces each send
to have one job, which is what stops a sequence turning into a newsletter.

**Range evidence:** the same four beats carry the autoresponder, AppSumo deal
copy, and a Craigslist camera-rental ad (@ 05:44). If it holds for classified
ads it will hold for your welcome sequence.

## 2. Sequence shapes

From the AI-assisted workflow lesson, but the shapes are format-independent
(*Drop Dead Copy, 03-John McIntyre AI Copy Secrets Vol 1* @ 49:07):

- **Welcome** — ~7 emails
- **Promotional launch** — ~5 emails
- **Customer onboarding** — ~20 emails

Treat these as starting lengths, not rules. The relevant constraint from that
lesson is that sequence design is "limited by what you can imagine and what you
can communicate" — the bottleneck is your clarity about each email's objective,
per §1.

## 3. Deliverability and formatting basics

Medhora on typeface: use a universal one. "Arial is the font we should be sending
our email in — generally that's accepted as a universal font that everyone has on
their machines. If you use some fancy font, some people may not have it"
(*01-Copywriting Course > 05-BONUS VIDEO* @ 33:47).

Dated in specifics, sound in principle: email clients are not browsers, and a
font that fails silently degrades your layout on someone else's machine.

## 3b. Preview text is a second subject line

Preview text is "the bit of text below or next to an email subject line in the
inbox, and it gives extra insight into what's inside the email." Citing Litmus:
**as much as 24% of respondents look at the preview text before deciding to
open** (*Product Marketing, 11-Competitive Intel  Market Research >
07-Competitive Newsletters for Product  the C-Suite* @ 11:07).

Most senders leave it to default to the first line of the email, which is
usually a greeting or a preheader instruction. That wastes roughly a quarter of
the open decision. Write it as a distinct second headline — not a restatement of
the subject, and not the start of the body.

The same lesson supplies a repeatable format for a recurring internal
newsletter, which generalises to any digest: competitor or topic as the title, a
**sub-10-word** summary line, and a **sub-1,000-character** body (@ 09:17).

## 4. Free things must actually be free

The most counter-intuitive finding for lead-magnet emails
(Schutz, *8. Pricing Psychology > 8.4 Special - Money, discounts & freebies*
@ 14:13):

> "if you give away things it should be **totally free**. Even asking a telephone
> number and email address — that feels like I'm giving you something in return.
> You shouldn't do that, because then the reciprocity is gone... and the effect is
> gone."

This cuts against standard list-building. Requiring an email address converts the
gift into a transaction, and the reciprocity effect you were counting on
disappears.

**It does not mean never gate anything.** It means: do not expect a gated asset to
generate goodwill. If you want reciprocity, give something ungated. If you want
addresses, gate — and pay for them in perceived value, not in expected warmth.

## 5. Discount timing — after, not before

From the same lesson: test "giving that free extra discount **after** people
bought, as a delightful moment which is unexpected — boosting their experience,
making them more loyal in the long run and **less price sensitive** because it was
such a cool experience" (@ 14:13).

This is a post-purchase email strategy with a specific claim attached: it reduces
future price sensitivity. Compare with pre-purchase discounting, which trains the
opposite.

**Related competitive move:** look at where competitors charge and make that part
free — "especially shipping and handling costs are very well known to be
effective. If you make that free you will beat any competitor that's charging
money there" (@ 31:52).

## 6. Promotional emails carry offer mechanics

Everything in `offers.md` applies inside the email, with one amplification:
scarcity in email is more likely to be repeated to the same person across a
sequence, and Schutz's warning about drip pricing — that repetition breeds "this
is not fair... they start disliking you for it" (*8.2* @ 32:47) — generalises.

A launch sequence that runs the same scarcity claim five times is testing the
reader's patience, not their urgency. If you use scarcity, it must be **reasoned**
and it must be **true** (`offers.md` §4).

---

## 7. Checklist

1. **Does every email have one named objective?** (§1)
2. **Are A / I / D / A actually present, in order?** (§1)
3. **Is the subject line opening a curiosity gap?** (`headlines.md` §1)
4. **If something is free, is it free with no strings?** (§4)
5. **Is your discount landing after purchase rather than before?** (§5)
6. **Does repeated scarcity across the sequence still hold up as true?** (§6)
7. **Will it render on someone else's machine?** (§3)
