# Frameworks

Named structures for organising copy, and the behavioural models underneath them.
Every claim carries its lesson and timestamp.

Two families live here and they answer different questions. **Copy formulas**
(AIDA and relatives) answer *what order do the words go in*. **Behavioural
models** (ELM, Fogg, Hooked) answer *what has to be true for the reader to act at
all*. Reach for the second when the first is not working.

---

## 1. AIDA — the default structure

**Attention, Interest, Desire, Action.** The corpus's most-cited structure by a
wide margin, and Medhora's working default: "I use it for everything I use it
like in stories I tell in person" (Medhora, *02-Copy Concepts > 02-The AIDA Model
What It Is and How to Use It* @ 00:00).

What makes it worth the cliché is its range. Medhora shows the same four beats
carrying an entire autoresponder sequence, AppSumo deal copy, and a Craigslist
camera-rental ad — "even if it's not a long-form sales copy" (*01-Videos >
244-Follow this AIDA formula for everything you write* @ 05:44).

His method is literally to write the four words down the page first and fill
them in: "I literally write out attention, interest, desire, action"
(*01-Copywriting Course > 05-BONUS VIDEO* @ 33:47).

**Use it when** you have a single offer and a reader who does not yet know they
want it. **Do not** stretch it over a page that has several unrelated jobs.

## 2. The story spine

The corpus converges on one structure from two directions.

**From behavioural science:** "your brain can't tell the difference between you
having a real experience and you having a pretend experience through a story" —
attributed on the recording to behavioural psychologist Susan Weinschenk
(*Product Marketing, 13-Radical Differentiation > 11-Message* @ 01:19). The same
lesson notes that "all stories tend to use the same structure, even copywriting
frameworks. A character has a problem, they meet a guide."

The full chain as the lesson states it, credited to Donald Miller's *Building a
StoryBrand*: **a character has a problem → they meet a guide → the guide gives
them a plan → that calls them to action → which prevents failure → and leads to
success** (@ 01:19).

That is the spine: **character → problem → guide → plan → outcome**. Your reader
is the character. You are the guide, never the hero.

**Substituted into positioning vocabulary**, which is how the same lesson
applies it:

> A character in your **minimum viable market** wants to solve a pain, reach a
> goal, or kill a monster. They meet your brand. You give them a plan that calls
> them to action. That helps them avoid staying in pain or missing the goal. At
> the end, they reach it.

The instructor demonstrates it on the course itself: "marketers and entrepreneurs
struggle to stand out — they struggle because no one, not even the experts,
explains how to actually do it" (@ 01:19).

Note how cleanly this maps onto `positioning.md`: the monster is the villain, the
goal is the promised land, and "the plan" is the only place features legitimately
enter. Three modules, one shape.

**From practice:** Morrison frames the whole job as belief transfer — "sales is
just a transference of belief," where the reader arrives at "this person gets
where I'm at and they know how to help me. I understand how this takes me from
where I am now to where I want to go" (*06-Bonus > 02-How to Write Sales Pages
That Actually Sell (part 1)* @ 02:38).

Note the agreement: the story spine is the *mechanism*, belief transfer is the
*outcome*. If a section advances neither, cut it.

## 3. Repetition and multi-channel messaging

Morrison's "Dora the Explorer" principle: "people just need repeated exposure to
the same thing. It's helping people's mind understand it again and again"
(*04-Messaging Best Practices > 07-Dora the Explorer Messaging* @ 04:10).

Her sharper point is that repetition should vary its *form*, not its content —
the same idea in registers that reach different readers. She runs an analogy
(caterpillar to butterfly) for emotional processors and a step-by-step process
for "my more analytical thinkers" (@ 04:10).

This maps directly onto dual-process theory (`psychology.md` §1): analogy is a
System 1 device, process is a System 2 device. Saying it once, one way, reaches
half your audience.

## 4. The five behavioural models

From Schutz, *1.3 Theoretical Models for System 2*. He is openly opinionated
about which deserve your time, and the disagreements are preserved here.

**Elaboration Likelihood Model** (Petty & Cacioppo, mid-80s) — central versus
peripheral route (@ 00:12). Schutz's respect for it is that it identified
**motivation and ability** as the two determinants decades before the current
models (@ 01:46). Still his working frame.

**Theory of Planned Behaviour** — **he is not a fan**, and does the arithmetic
aloud: its determinants predict intention 40–50%, intention predicts behaviour
20–40%, so roughly 10–15% real predictive power (@ 03:50). "That's not a lot. It
means that there's 85 to 90% other factors." He keeps only self-efficacy.

**Reflective–Impulsive Model** — valued as the first to model *interaction*
between the two systems rather than treating them as alternative routes (@ 07:05).

**BJ Fogg's behaviour model** — motivation × ability determines which trigger you
need (@ 07:40). The practical payoff, in his words:

- Motivation high, ability high → a **signalling** trigger is enough.
- Motivation low → a **sparking** trigger: "make them crave more for it or induce
  more regret" (@ 09:02).
- Ability low → a **facilitating** trigger: reduce effort or raise self-efficacy
  (@ 09:17).
- Either too low → **no trigger works** (@ 09:27).

Diagnose which one you are short of before rewriting. Most weak CTAs are an
ability problem being treated as a motivation problem.

**Nir Eyal's Hooked** — the model for moving a decision *out* of deliberation and
into habit: external trigger → action in anticipation of reward → **variable**
reward → investment, which seeds the next loop (@ 11:48). The variability is the
load-bearing part, drawn from Skinner: reward every time and behaviour stops when
the reward stops; reward variably and it persists (@ 13:06). Investment works
because "if you invest in something, you like it better" (@ 14:12).

**Scope limit, stated by Schutz:** Hooked applies to repeated small decisions —
SaaS, apps — and is "less applicable if you are trying to make people buy one
time a big product" (@ 15:24).

## 5. The toothbrush problem — read this before adopting any model

Schutz's warning about the entire category: scientists are rewarded for being
cited, so nobody adopts anyone else's model. Everyone builds a small variant on a
shared base (*1.1 Introduction System 1 & 2* @ 23:05).

His worked example: BJ Fogg's model is substantially the triade model his own
professor taught in the early 90s, with "trigger" substituted for "opportunity"
(@ 23:36).

**The instruction that follows:** find the *overlap* between models, because
overlap is what is actually validated. Motivation and ability recur in every one
of the five above — that is the signal. A construct appearing in exactly one
framework is that author's differentiator, not established science.

Applied to copy: build on motivation and ability. Treat any single framework's
proprietary vocabulary as branding.

## 6. Operational frameworks

**Content cadence** (Fox). A month gets one broad topic, split into four
sub-topics, one per week: "within offer, we have niche messaging, offer building
and pricing" (*Magnetic Content 2.0 > 03-Live SOP and Strategy* @ 19:54). Solves
the blank-page problem structurally rather than by inspiration.

**Funnel selection by price point** (Fox). A sales-page funnel is "exclusively
for someone who is willing to sell a product under a thousand dollars," while
higher tickets route through free training and a booked call (*X Twitter Profit >
04-Twitter Funnels Setup* @ 05:10). Choose the funnel from the price, not from
preference.

**Template caveat, his own:** people take the template and change only the
photos. "Make sure it's your colors, your branding... as you as possible"
(@ 19:54). Applies to every framework on this page.

**Segment building** (Product Marketing). A five-step survey-design process
beginning with preliminary research into which attributes are worth fielding for
(*07-Segmentation and Persona Research > 05-How to Build Segments* @ 01:22).
Deliberately assumes no special technology. See `research.md`.

**Theme coding** (Product Marketing). Content research is treated as qualitative
coding — break the data into themes, distil, then map themes onto topics
(*01-Content Marketing Research > 05-Identifying themes and topics* @ 00:16).

**Demand type before structure** (Product Marketing). Which framework fits
depends on whether the buyer has a reference point at all: a new concept has to
teach before it can sell, an established category has to differentiate
(*12-Positioning > 05-How to Identify Your Demand Type* @ 00:00). Run this before
§8, not after — see `positioning.md` §4.

## 7. Research → organise → edit

Joanna Wiebe's three-step process closes the gap between research and a usable
draft:

1. **Research and discovery.** This is "the biggest part" of writing; the job is
   to listen for the message rather than invent it
   (*13-Radical Differentiation > 11-everyone-hates-marketers__3-steps-to-write-copy-tha*
   @ 07:45).
2. **Organise the evidence inside a framework.** Put customer language into PAS,
   AIDA, or another structure in a wireframe or document. The organised research
   replaces the blank page (@ 31:38).
3. **Edit the assembled language into copy.** Wiebe calls this "edit in the
   awesome": voice-edit and clarify the material until it is ready to test,
   without rebuilding it from scratch (@ 46:10–47:27).

The framework is selected *after* research. If the evidence does not show a
strong pain, do not force PAS; choose a structure that fits what the market
actually said (@ 30:44). This is the operational bridge between `research.md`
and every formula in this file.

## 8. Choosing between them

| Situation | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Single offer, cold reader | AIDA (§1) |
| Reader must change how they see themselves | Story spine + belief transfer (§2) |
| Message is not landing across your audience | Repetition in varied registers (§3) |
| Traffic arrives but does not act | Fogg — diagnose motivation vs ability (§4) |
| One-time purchase, high consideration | ELM central route: evidence and argument |
| Recurring product, retention problem | Hooked (§4), within its stated scope |
| Choosing a model at all | Toothbrush problem (§5) — trust the overlap |
| Nobody has budgeted for this category yet | Demand type (§6) — teach before you sell |
| You have interviews but no draft | Research → organise → edit (§7) |

## 9. New-source additions: choose the reasoning shape before the copy shape

Barbara Minto's pyramid is a useful companion to AIDA: state the governing
answer first, group supporting ideas logically, and place evidence under the
reason it supports. It is especially useful for briefs, sales enablement, and
long-form copy whose subheads should carry the argument. (*The Minto Pyramid
Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking, and Problem Solving*.)

For experiments, Croll and Yoskovitz's Lean Analytics distinction keeps the
framework honest: choose an actionable metric, avoid vanity metrics, and
separate correlated signals from causal tests. A framework is not validated
because the dashboard moved; the decision and the measurement design have to
match. (*Lean Analytics*, pages 38 and 55.)

**Selection rule:** use the simplest structure that lets the reader follow the
promise, reasons, proof, and next action. Use the pyramid to organise the
argument; use AIDA or the story spine to control movement; use a test plan to
learn whether the message worked.
