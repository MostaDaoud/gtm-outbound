# Offers & Pricing

Structuring the ask. Every claim carries its lesson and timestamp.

Most of the rigorous material here comes from Schutz's pricing-psychology
sections, which are unusual in the corpus for reporting the *limits* of each
tactic alongside the tactic.

---

## 1. Structure before number

The order is explicit: choose a **price structure** first, then set the **base
price** within it (*8. Pricing Psychology > 8.2 Setting your price* @ 08:10).
Three structures are available:

- **Single fixed price.**
- **Complex pricing** — combining products so they resist direct comparison.
- **Drip pricing** — a headline price with increments disclosed later
  (base price, then shipping and handling, etc.).

Deciding the structure first matters because it determines what the number even
means. A base price optimised inside the wrong structure is optimised against the
wrong comparison set.

**And one step earlier still: establish the demand type.** Every structure above
assumes the buyer has something to compare you against. For a **new concept** —
genuinely novel, no reference points — the product marketing curriculum notes
there is usually **no budget allocated at all**, and the job is explaining what
it does and why they should care (*Product Marketing, 12-Positioning >
05-How to Identify Your Demand Type* @ 00:00).

Anchoring, decoys and comparison tactics all presuppose an established category.
In a new concept they have nothing to bite on. See `positioning.md` §4.

### The reference point is the hidden variable

Buyers do not evaluate a price or outcome in isolation. Prospect theory adds
the earlier state against which a result is experienced as a gain or a loss
(Kahneman — *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, @ 13:19:00). Before choosing the anchor,
write down the buyer's current reference point: the status quo, the expected
category price, the existing contract, or the option they already possess.

That also limits how casually to use the endowment effect. Kahneman notes that
it can disappear when the reference point changes, and that experienced traders
who treat goods as inventory are less susceptible (@ 14:05:00; @ 16:10:30).
Ownership is not a universal pricing lever; it depends on what the buyer thinks
they are giving up.

## 2. Drip pricing works by anchoring — and has a hard limit

**Why it works:** "you see a first element of the price up front — you're going to
pay $200 for this Dell laptop — and you're showing price increments later, but
the anchor is 200." Comparison then runs against $200, and although the
increments are disclosed honestly, "we adjust the price too little. The anchor is
so much defining" (*8.2 Setting your price* @ 25:18).

**Where it breaks, in his own words:** with repeated increments the buyer gets
"a feeling of this is not fair, this is going too far... how much am I going to
pay in the end," and "they start disliking you for it" (*8.2* @ 32:47).

**The fix he gives:** combine additions into **one single surcharge** rather than
several. Or show the total clearly and persistently — "a pricing ticket on the
right-hand side of your checkout process" that stays in view — so the buyer never
feels manipulated (@ 32:47).

**Carry this caveat forward.** Drip pricing degrades with repetition, which makes
it a poor fit for repeat-purchase businesses where the same buyer meets it
repeatedly. The anchoring gain is one-shot; the resentment compounds.

## 3. Reducing the pain of paying

A distinct problem from setting the price: the buyer has accepted the number and
still has to hand it over. Two levers are named — **how the payment is timed**,
and the framing around it (*8.3 Reducing the pain of paying* @ 00:00).

Timing is the underused one. Payment separated in time from consumption hurts
less than payment at the moment of use.

## 4. Scarcity — the full recipe, not the shortcut

Scarcity is the most-abused tactic in copywriting, and the corpus is precise
about what it actually requires (*7. Persuasive Marketing > 7.2 Techniques for
Your Offer* @ 33:57):

**Three components that make it real:**
- Limited availability
- **Reasoned** scarcity — a stated reason why it is limited
- Active competition — evidence others are pursuing it

**Two additions the studies require:**
- An **extrinsic motivator**, typically a discount, alongside the scarcity
  (*7.1 Your Offer* @ 25:51 and *7.2* @ 33:57). Booking.com is cited as having
  tested this exhaustively — "you will see a lot of discounts or the illusion of
  discounts applied as well."
- **Emphasise the loss, not the gain.** Losing it "is more emotional than the
  gain of acting now and getting the product before it's sold out" (@ 33:57).

Kahneman supplies the broader mechanism and its boundary: losses often receive
roughly twice the weight of gains across gambles, endowment, and reactions to
price changes, but the coefficient varies by situation (*Thinking, Fast and
Slow*, @ 16:40:00). Use the customer's real reference point and real loss. The
finding does not license inventing a loss that does not exist.

**Why the vagueness helps.** On platforms where "you cannot calculate the scarcity,"
the feeling still lands "because of the supersizing of base rate neglect. We find
it very hard to say, so many people — of how many?" (@ 33:57).

**Read that alongside the ethics line.** Schutz refuses to demonstrate base-rate
neglect elsewhere, calling it "pure manipulation" (*1.4* @ 18:07). The mechanism
that makes uncountable scarcity work is the same one he declines to exploit
deliberately. Reasoned, true scarcity is the defensible version; invented
counters are the line.

## 5. Challenge your own explanation of why it worked

The most methodologically valuable passage in the section. A test showed
"1267 people bought this product" lifting conversion — apparently social proof.
On investigation, the lift came from **the number itself**, not the proof: replace
it with "most popular" or "most bought" and the effect disappears, while showing
an unrelated product with that same figure as a *price* reproduced it
(*8.3 Reducing the pain of paying* @ 29:41).

His instruction: "if we really want to learn about customer behavior we should
challenge those alternative explanations" (@ 29:41).

Applied to offers: a winning test tells you *that* something moved, not *why*.
Ship the winner, but do not build a doctrine on the explanation you assumed.

## 6. Sell the end state, not the instrument

From live client work on online loans: "the more we emphasize the end goal —
which is a new kitchen or a new car — and the less emphasis we put on the loan
itself, the higher the conversion rate. Taking away the word credits, taking away
the word loan, making the products less comparable — just apply for it, we'll
call you, we'll make sure you get the money" (*4. Need & Motivations >
4.5 Techniques* @ 37:57).

Three moves in one result: **name the outcome**, **remove the category noun**,
and **reduce comparability**. The last connects back to complex pricing in §1 —
comparison is the enemy of margin.

## 7. Decoys and tiers

The "ugly brother": adding a slightly worse near-twin shifts choice toward the
better one (*1.4 System 2 Persuasion Techniques* @ 19:57).

The real case is worth copying exactly: Usabilla raised the price of its
**smallest** plan from 50 to 80 against a 90 middle tier. Conversion stayed flat
while **average order value rose**, because the small plan became an ugly brother
of the middle one (@ 23:10).

Note the shape — they did not add a decoy, they *made an existing tier worse
value*. Cheaper to implement and less cluttered than a fourth column.

**Constraint from choice paradox** (@ 24:24): three to five options, and chunk if
you exceed that. But Schutz's own testing found choice paradox does not apply to
System 1 purchases at all — they never found a ceiling on shoes shown to women or
RC cars shown to men. Tier discipline matters for considered purchases; it may
not for impulse ones.

## 8. The Value Equation and offer enhancers

Hormozi's primary offer model is a four-part tradeoff: increase the **dream
outcome** and the **perceived likelihood of achievement**; decrease the
perceived **time delay** and **effort/sacrifice** required to reach it. He
introduces those four drivers as a way to inspect value before choosing the
price or writing the pitch (Alex Hormozi — *$100M Offers*, chapter 4, “Value —
Create Your Offer”).

Use the model as a diagnostic, not a promise generator:

1. State the end state in the customer's language.
2. List the proof, process, and constraints that make achievement believable.
3. Remove avoidable waiting, steps, and hidden costs.
4. Make the copy show which parts of the equation the offer actually improves.

Hormozi makes the hidden-cost point again in *$100M Leads*: when a free asset
still feels expensive, the obstacle may be the time, effort, or sacrifice needed
to use it, not its price (Alex Hormozi — *$100M Leads*, page 97). Treat that as
an author-guided hypothesis and test the friction with real prospects; do not
copy the book's illustrative economics as proof.

### Enhance only what can be true

The offer-enhancement chapter separates three often-blended levers:

- **Scarcity** limits quantity; **urgency** limits time. State the actual
  constraint rather than using a countdown as decoration (Alex Hormozi —
  *$100M Offers*, chapter 5, “Enhancing Your Offer,” parts 23 and 34).
- A useful **bonus** addresses a specific objection or next obstacle. Tools and
  checklists can reduce time and effort, but the bonus still needs a real job;
  its claimed value is not evidence merely because a price tag is printed on it
  (Alex Hormozi — *$100M Offers*, chapter 5, “Enhancing Your Offer,” page 33).
- A strong **guarantee** states the result, the time window, and what happens if
  the result is not achieved. “Guaranteed” without the consequence is not risk
  reversal (Alex Hormozi — *$100M Offers*, chapter 5, “Enhancing Your Offer,”
  page 42).

LinkedIn Lab's integrity rule still controls: scarcity, urgency, guarantees, prices,
and outcomes must be operationally real and supported by the user's evidence.

### Derivative prompt aid

Ben Preston's *100M Offers Made Easy* turns the same four drivers into a
structured prompt for brainstorming and comparison. It is useful as an AI
workflow aid, but it is a derivative interpretation: its numerical “offer
scores” are heuristics, not measurements of conversion probability, and its
voice-emulation framing is not a substitute for the client's voice (Ben Preston
— *100M Offers Made Easy*, chapter “Offer Ideation”).

## 9. Checklist

1. **Structure chosen before number?** (§1)
2. **What reference point makes this price a gain or loss?** (§1)
3. **If dripping, is it one surcharge and is the total always visible?** (§2)
4. **Is this a repeat-purchase business?** If so, reconsider drip entirely. (§2)
5. **Is your scarcity reasoned and true?** Does it carry a discount and a loss
   frame? (§4)
6. **Have you challenged the alternative explanation for your last winning
   test?** (§5)
7. **Does the copy name the end state rather than the instrument?** (§6)
8. **Three to five tiers, with the relative-value story deliberate?** (§7)

## 10. New-source additions: price the value the buyer can recognize

### Price the client and the outcome, not the task

Alan Weiss argues that a value-based fee is compensation for the client's
agreed improved condition, not a disguised time sheet. Blair Enns makes the
same distinction for creative work: the same job can be worth different amounts
to different clients, and the price conversation should precede solution
design when possible. Scope reductions should remove value as well as price.
(*Value-Based Fees*, pages 34, 290, 374, and 381; *Pricing Creativity*, chapters
3, 5, 7, and 23.)

**linkedin-lab application:** name the buyer's improved condition, the evidence that
the buyer values it, and what changes between tiers. Never imply that a higher
fee is justified merely because more hours were spent.

### Translate willingness-to-pay into the offer

Ramanujam and Tacke recommend asking about pain and valued outcomes before
asking about an acceptable price; the sequence makes willingness-to-pay easier
to discuss. Nagle, Hogan, and Zale separate reference value (the customer's
next-best alternative) from use value (the differentiated benefit), then require
the copy to communicate why the premium is fair. Simon reduces the same idea to
customer-perceived value: willingness-to-pay rises or falls with perceived value.
(*Monetizing Innovation*, pages 64, 120, 160, and 167; *The Strategy and
Tactics of Pricing*, chapters 1, 2, and “Price and Value Communication
Strategies”; *Confessions of the Pricing Man*, chapter “Everything Revolves
Around Price”.)

Build the price story in this order: alternative/reference point → outcome and
proof → differentiated value → price and tradeoff. Do not hide a price change
behind a feature list.

### Metrics are decision instruments

Croll and Yoskovitz distinguish actionable metrics from vanity metrics and
correlated from causal measures; Farris, Bendle, Pfeifer, and Reibstein add
customer profitability and retention/LTV views. Use the smallest metric set
that changes a decision, and keep acquisition, retention, and customer
profitability in the same frame. (*Lean Analytics*, pages 38, 55, and 279;
*Marketing Metrics*, pages 147–159.)

These books provide measurement frameworks, not proof of a client's results.
Any number used in copy still needs the client's source, segment, timeframe,
and denominator.
