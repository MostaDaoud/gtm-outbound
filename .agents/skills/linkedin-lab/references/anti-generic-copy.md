# Anti-generic copy pass

Use this reference when the user asks to make copy sound less AI-like, less
generic, more natural, or more like a supplied human voice. It is a
reader-facing editing pass, not an authorship test and not a detector-evasion
system.

---

## 1. Scope and provenance

This pass adapts the useful reader-mode material from a user-supplied
anti-AI skill: recurring constructions, over-planned structure, stock diction,
uniform rhythm, and missing first-hand detail. Treat these as editing
heuristics, not scientific proof that a model wrote a passage.

Do not import that skill's detector recipes. LinkedIn Lab does not deliberately
damage discourse, insert fake mistakes, optimize against a classifier, or
promise that edited copy will pass an AI detector. Detector verdicts are
unstable, can misclassify human work, and are separate from whether a reader
finds the copy credible and distinctive.

The governing idea is:

**Preserve the author's voice and facts. Remove clustered generic patterns.**

---

## 2. When to run the pass

Run it when:

- The user says “humanize this,” “make this sound less AI,” “remove AI
  clichés,” or “this feels generic.”
- The user supplies a voice sample and asks for a rewrite.
- A critique finds polished but interchangeable language.
- A final draft contains several patterns below in close proximity.

Do not run the full pass merely because one common word or construction
appears. One occurrence proves nothing; clustering is the useful signal.

---

## 3. Protect before editing

Write down what must survive:

1. The message and intended action.
2. Every verified fact, number, name, and claim.
3. The author's actual experience and unusual phrasing.
4. The required format and channel.
5. Any line with genuine personality or persuasive force.

When the user supplies a voice sample, derive the target from that sample:
sentence length, contractions, vocabulary, attitude, humor, and degree of
formality. Do not replace a real voice with a generic “casual” house style.

---

## 4. Audit top-down

### A. Structure

Look for:

- An opening that restates the assignment before making a point.
- A rigid introduction, three equally weighted points, and recap.
- Previews and summaries repeated at several levels.
- The same idea restated in slightly different words.
- A conclusion beginning “in conclusion,” “in summary,” or “ultimately” and
  ending with an inspirational lift.
- An anecdote polished into a suspiciously perfect lesson.

Fix:

- Start where the claim, scene, or tension starts.
- Say each important idea once, where it lands hardest.
- Let sections have different lengths.
- End on the last concrete point, image, decision, or instruction.
- Keep a true unresolved edge when the source material has one.

### B. Repeated constructions

Flag clusters of:

- Symmetrical contrast: “It is not X; it is Y.”
- Repeated three-item lists.
- Rhetorical question followed immediately by its answer.
- Drumroll phrases such as “here is the kicker” or “the best part?”
- Vague authority such as “experts say” or “studies show.”
- Participial tails that announce significance instead of making a claim.
- Inflated copulas such as “serves as,” “stands as,” or “represents.”
- An analogy for every abstract idea.
- Grand labels coined for ordinary problems.

Fix:

- State the positive claim directly.
- Keep the strongest items rather than forcing a trio.
- Keep only questions a reader might genuinely ask.
- Deliver the information without announcing it.
- Name the source, own the opinion, or cut the claim.
- Prefer “is” and “has” when they say the same thing.

These constructions are not universally banned. Deliberate repetition,
contrast, and rhythm can be excellent copy. The problem is automatic,
same-shaped repetition that makes every paragraph feel generated from one
template.

### C. Rhythm and formatting

Look for:

- Sentences that all occupy the same narrow length.
- A chain of short punchline fragments.
- Rectangular paragraphs of equal size.
- Too many one-sentence paragraphs.
- Repeated em-dash pivots.
- Emoji bullets, bold-label bullets, or Markdown residue that does not belong
  in the destination.
- A generic title-case headline split by a colon.

Fix:

- Vary sentence and paragraph length according to meaning.
- Use a short sentence because it deserves emphasis, not as a house style.
- Keep the punctuation and formatting habits of the supplied voice sample.
- Remove markup that will not render in the final channel.

Do not enforce a mechanical quota such as “one em dash” across every brand.
Pattern density and fit matter more than a universal count.

### D. Diction

Common generic clusters include:

- leverage, utilize, harness, streamline, empower
- robust, seamless, transformative, game-changing
- landscape, ecosystem, journey, tapestry
- navigate the complexities, shed light on, valuable insights
- in today's fast-paced world, it is important to note
- furthermore, moreover, additionally at the start of successive sentences

Replace them with:

- The plain word the author would say aloud.
- A concrete noun from the product or reader's world.
- The specific change, action, or result the adjective was hiding.

Do not operate a blind banned-word list. “Framework,” “strategy,” “critical,”
and similar words may be exactly right in technical or professional copy. A
cluster of abstract terms is the finding; a necessary term of art is not.

### E. Evidence and lived material

Ask:

- Can the reader picture anything in the first few sentences?
- Is there a true name, number, place, product detail, or moment?
- Does the copy contain language a customer or this author actually used?
- Does the story install a belief, or merely decorate the page?
- Is every authority or performance claim sourced?

The strongest correction is usually real material: voice-of-customer language,
a verified result, a product constraint, or a first-hand moment.

Never invent a client, date, price, statistic, quotation, sensory detail, or
personal experience to make text appear human. Use a precise marker instead:

- [REAL EXAMPLE NEEDED: what happened]
- [SPECIFIC NEEDED: verified number or detail]
- [SOURCE NEEDED: study, customer, or internal data]

---

## 5. Rewrite sequence

1. Preserve the message, facts, format, and strongest voice lines.
2. Cut prompt echoes, throat-clearing, duplicate explanation, and recap.
3. Replace unsupported abstraction with a verified specific or a marker.
4. Break repeated construction patterns without flattening deliberate rhetoric.
5. Match rhythm and register to the author and channel.
6. Read the result aloud and rewrite anything the author would not naturally
   say.
7. Recheck the offer, proof, and call to action; a voice pass must not weaken
   conversion logic.

---

## 6. Critique integration

Do not add a sixth category or an “AI score.”

Map findings into the existing rubric:

- Repetitive structure and unclear abstraction → Category 2.
- Unsupported specifics or vague authority → Category 3.
- Interchangeable diction, rhythm, and missing author material → Category 4.

Score the commercial or comprehension effect, not the number of stylistic
tells. Quote the failing line and provide a rewrite.

Never infer authorship. Say “this passage uses clustered generic patterns,” not
“AI wrote this.”

---

## 7. Delivery rules

When the user asks for a rewrite:

1. Return the clean rewrite first.
2. Preserve placeholders for facts the user must supply.
3. Add a short change note only when requested or when a missing fact affects
   credibility.

When the user asks for an audit:

1. Name the target register.
2. Quote the clustered patterns.
3. Explain their reader effect.
4. Rewrite the important lines.
5. Preserve two or more lines that already sound distinctive.

If the user asks for detector-proof text, say that LinkedIn Lab can improve
reader-facing specificity and voice but cannot guarantee or verify detector
classification.

---

## 8. Final checklist

- [ ] The opening starts with the point, scene, or tension.
- [ ] No repeated preview-and-recap structure.
- [ ] No cluster of same-shaped rhetorical constructions.
- [ ] Sentence and paragraph rhythm follows meaning.
- [ ] Abstract claims have real support or explicit markers.
- [ ] The language matches the supplied author or brand sample.
- [ ] Distinctive lines survived the edit.
- [ ] No facts, experiences, mistakes, or quirks were fabricated.
- [ ] The offer, proof, and action remain clear.
- [ ] No claim is made about who wrote the text or whether a detector will pass it.
