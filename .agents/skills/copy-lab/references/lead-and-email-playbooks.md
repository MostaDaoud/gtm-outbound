# Lead, Headline, Outreach, and Email Playbooks

Use this reference for welcome sequences, lead activation, consulting outreach,
sales-email QA, long-form message architecture, formula-driven headline
ideation, and acquisition-channel planning. It distills five page-indexed local
PDFs plus Alex Hormozi's *$100M Leads*:

- Csaba Borzasi — *The Rapid Lead Activator*
- Csaba Borzasi — *Secret Copy Recipe*
- Game of Conversions — *The Ultimate Email Checklist*
- Game of Conversions — *5-Part Consulting Email Sequence*
- Game of Conversions — *39 Proven Headline Formulas*

These sources are tactical playbooks, not independent experimental validation.
Treat performance claims as the authors' claims unless the user supplies test
data. Copy Lab's proof and scarcity rules override every template.

## 1. The five-email lead-activation sequence

Borzasi assigns one strategic job to each email:

| Email | Job | Primary action |
|---:|---|---|
| 1. Trust-Builder Welcome | Deliver the promised asset, set expectations, introduce positioning | Download, then a simple reply |
| 2. Conversation Starter | Learn who joined and surface high-intent prospects | Reply to one simple question |
| 3. Origin Story | Build emotional context for the author's motivation and method | Reply or consume a relevant asset |
| 4. Credo | State what the brand stands for and against | Engage with the belief or relevant links |
| 5. Reason Why Different & Superior | Explain the distinct mechanism and next step | Reply or visit the next-step offer |

The sequence and each email's role are summarized on page 37; its CTA map
appears on page 95. (Csaba Borzasi — *The Rapid Lead Activator*, pages 37 and
95)

### Email 1: fulfill before selling

The welcome email should immediately deliver the signup promise, establish what
the subscriber will receive, disclose that relevant offers may appear, and ask
for a low-friction response. (Borzasi, pages 39–43)

Use the reply as a genuine conversation invitation, not a disguised consent to
hard-pitch.

### Email 2: ask a S.P.E.A.R. question

Borzasi defines the conversation starter as **simple, personal, expecting a
response**. Ask one question that reveals the subscriber's situation and can
help route them toward suitable material or an offer. The source explicitly
warns against treating the response as permission for an immediate hard pitch.
(Borzasi, pages 45–46)

Examples of the question's *shape*:

- “What kind of clients do you work with?”
- “Which part of [process] is slowing you down?”
- “What are you hoping to change first?”

### Emails 3–5: story, belief, mechanism

The origin story explains why the author cares and how the method emerged; the
credo names the beliefs that attract like-minded readers; the final email shifts
from the author's beliefs to why the method itself differs from alternatives.
(Borzasi, pages 48, 72, and 84)

Keep the mechanism concrete. “Our proprietary approach” is not differentiation;
name the causal step that changes the result and support comparative claims.

## 2. Five-part consulting outreach

The consulting sequence progresses from permission to diagnosis to a call:

1. **Primary outreach:** show a specific observation, its likely consequence,
   relevant ability to help, and ask permission to send ideas. (Game of
   Conversions — *5-Part Consulting Email Sequence*, page 2)
2. **Second attempt:** restate the problem and desired outcome more compactly,
   then repeat the low-friction permission ask. (page 3)
3. **After a positive reply:** present three tailored improvement areas, connect
   features to benefits, and offer a fit-check conversation. (pages 4–5)
4. **Reminder:** ask whether the problem is still active and repeat the booking
   step. (page 6)
5. **Close-the-loop email:** state that this is the final follow-up and leave a
   clear next step. (page 7)

### Integrity corrections

The source templates include held spots, short deadlines, large result claims,
and testimonial slots. Never carry those into output automatically:

- use a held spot or deadline only when it is operationally real;
- use a multiplier or timeframe only when supplied evidence supports it;
- use a testimonial only when the user provides the wording and permission;
- a final follow-up may be final because the sequence ends, without inventing a
  disappearing offer.

## 3. Email QA: one email, one job

The checklist's strongest organizing rule is one goal per email. It recommends
one CTA *type*, which may appear more than once when the email is long, plus a
personal register, a clear hook, and an explicit action. (Game of Conversions —
*The Ultimate Email Checklist*, page 2)

### Subject line contract

A subject line should tease rather than summarize, visually fit the inbox, and
accurately represent what the email delivers. Curiosity that the body does not
pay off is clickbait, not a successful open loop. (Game of Conversions — *The
Ultimate Email Checklist*, page 3)

### Body and sender checks

The body should be scannable, move from hook through desire to action, use the
audience's own language, and keep the transition into the offer smooth. The
source presents AIDA and PASOP as optional flow structures. (pages 4–5)

Treat its unsupported statements about what “always” increases sales or open
rates as author guidance, not established fact.

## 4. Long-form “Ultimate Persuasive Message” skeleton

*Secret Copy Recipe* begins upstream: research the reader, awareness,
sophistication, competitors, and evidence; then choose one emotionally charged,
simple Big Idea. (Csaba Borzasi — *Secret Copy Recipe*, page 3)

Its full message architecture is:

1. Open: headline, market/problem callout, promise, mechanism tease, proof.
2. Emotional connection: credibility, discovery story, belief reframes.
3. Unique mechanism: explain the difference, restate the promise, prove it.
4. Product-creation story: connect the mechanism to the product's origin.
5. Product reveal: positioning, benefits, features, proof, bonuses.
6. Close: value, guarantee, action, genuine urgency, recap.
7. FAQ and objections. (Borzasi, pages 4–6)

The source then compresses the architecture for shorter letters, video ads,
social ads, and sales emails, preserving the same causal spine rather than
blindly shrinking every section. (Borzasi, pages 7–10)

Use the framework diagnostically, not as a requirement to manufacture a hero
story, false beliefs, prototype success, bonuses, or scarcity. Omit any element
the product cannot support.

## 5. Headline formula families

The 39-formula deck is best used as a variation generator after research, not as
a claim generator. Its patterns fall into reusable families:

| Family | Purpose | Source pages |
|---|---|---:|
| Audience callout | Signal “this is for people like me” | 2, 6–7 |
| Warning or mistake | Surface a costly error or risk | 2, 8–9 |
| Reveal, secret, or truth | Open a gap around little-known information | 2–5 |
| How-to with constraint removal | Pair a result with “even if” or “without” | 3–7 |
| Transformation | Move from an unwanted state to a desired one | 4–6 |
| Demonstration or challenge | Invite the reader to see or test something | 7–8 |
| Guarantee or direct promise | Put the outcome and risk reversal up front | 8 |

(Game of Conversions — *39 Proven Headline Formulas*, pages 2–9)

The word “proven” belongs to the source title. It does not mean a formula is
validated for the user's offer, audience, or traffic source. Any number,
timeframe, guarantee, expert status, or outcome inserted into a formula still
requires evidence.

## 6. Lead acquisition: the core four and lead magnets

Hormozi groups the main acquisition activities into four channels: **warm
outreach, free content, cold outreach, and paid ads** (Alex Hormozi — *$100M
Leads*, pages 6 and 252). Treat that as a coverage map, not a command to run
all four at once. Pick the channel whose audience, proof, and operating capacity
you can actually support, then add a second channel when it genuinely improves
the first.

The book also argues that a lead magnet can lower the perceived cost of engaging
with an ad or call by delivering value before the core offer (page 70). The
copy task is to make the asset solve a real first problem and make the next step
obvious; the illustrative acquisition math in the chapter is not a benchmark
for a new campaign. Test cost per engaged lead, qualified conversations, and
sales separately.

Finally, the “lead getters” section names referrals, employees, agencies, and
affiliates as ways other people can run those same acquisition activities on a
business's behalf (pages 249 and 350). Build the core-four message and offer
first; then decide whether another person can carry it without changing the
promise or proof.

## Combined workflow

1. Start with reader research and the one action.
2. Select the sequence or message architecture that fits the relationship:
   subscriber, cold prospect, or active buyer.
3. Give every email or section one strategic job.
4. Generate headline variants from two or three fitting families.
5. Attach every claim to supplied proof; remove unsupported superlatives.
6. Make every curiosity promise pay off in the body or product.
7. Use real operational timing for reminders and closes.
8. Measure replies, qualified conversations, clicks, and sales separately so a
   high open rate does not disguise a weak commercial result.

## Critique checks

| Check | Failure signal |
|---|---|
| Sequence job | Two emails perform the same job while another job is missing |
| Permission | A low-friction reply is treated as consent for a hard pitch |
| Relevance | Outreach observation could be sent to any prospect |
| Subject payoff | The body does not resolve the subject line's tease |
| CTA coherence | Several unrelated actions compete in one email |
| Mechanism | Differentiation is a label without a causal explanation |
| Proof | Formula placeholders became unsupported facts |
| Scarcity | A held spot, deadline, or final chance is not real |
| Fit | The source template is copied despite the user's different offer or channel |
