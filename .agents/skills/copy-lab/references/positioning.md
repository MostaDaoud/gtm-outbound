# Positioning

Deciding what you are, to whom, against what alternative — before any copy gets
written. Every claim carries its lesson and timestamp.

Most weak copy is a positioning failure wearing a copy costume. If the page has
to explain why the category matters, the problem is upstream of the words.

---

## 1. The competitor is the status quo

The single most useful reframe in the corpus:

> "you no longer compete against the competition you're competing against the
> status quo and in the modern landscape it's the status quo not your competitors
> that's the biggest impediment to change people are already doing something —
> even if that's that they're doing nothing"
> (*Product Marketing, 12-Positioning > 02-The 8 Elements of Your Positioning
> Story* @ 02:58)

The goal is stated as "making ourselves the only one that a certain kind of
person is going to consider" (@ 02:58). Note the two moves: *changing the
conversation*, and *narrowing who it is for*. Both are required — being the only
option for everyone is not available.

**What this changes in practice.** You must convince the reader to *change*, not
that you are best-in-class. Comparison tables answer the wrong question when the
reader has not yet decided to move at all.

The worked example makes the discipline concrete: a Drift page uses stakes,
social proof ("50,000 other businesses are growing faster"), and a call to
action — and never mentions Intercom (*12-Positioning > 08-How to Create a
Memorable Simple Promise* @ 10:18). Positioning against the status quo means
your competitor's name does not appear.

## 2. Positioning, messaging, and value proposition are different things

The course is explicit that these get conflated and shouldn't be
(*04-Positioning  Company Storytelling > 02-Stories Convey Meaning* @ 01:54).
Working distinction as taught:

- **Positioning** — the strategic choice of where you stand and for whom.
- **Value proposition** — the specific promise of gain.
- **Messaging** — the words that carry it to a particular audience.

They nest in that order. Writing messaging before positioning is settled produces
copy that reads fine and converts badly, because every sentence is arguing from
an unstated and possibly wrong premise.

### Turn the value proposition into a choice reason

The added interview sharpens the working definition: a value proposition is a
clear statement of why the buyer should choose you over *all* viable options,
including an existing workaround—not merely why you beat a named product
competitor (*13-Radical Differentiation >
11-everyone-hates-marketers__value-proposition-what-is* @ 03:37).

Build it from the overlap between what prospects say they want and what paying
customers say they actually received. Petdoors.com found demand for durability
and weather-tightness and customer evidence that those benefits were delivered;
the prior website copy had interrupted that match (@ 44:37–45:24). The strategic
test is therefore: **wanted outcome + delivered outcome + credible reason to
choose this option**.

### Build the promise through a benefit ladder

The RiseUp workshop adds a practical bridge between product facts and that
choice reason. Separate the promise into three layers before trying to write one
sentence:

| Layer | Customer-language prompt | What it must trace back to |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | “When I use this, I get…” | A real feature, attribute, or capability |
| **Emotional** | “When I use this, I feel…” | A plausible effect of the functional result |
| **Self-expressive** | “When I use this, I am…” | An identity, value, status, or affiliation the product helps express |

(Muhmorsi — *How to Craft Your Brand Value Proposition*, pages 10–12)

Do the work in five passes:

1. **Inventory the product facts.** Include how it works, specifications,
   problems solved, time or effort saved, ease of use, and relevant sensory or
   packaging attributes. (Muhmorsi, page 23)
2. **Translate facts into functional outcomes.** Ask “so what?” and “what can
   the customer do now?” Start with a verb, address the reader directly, and
   replace generic benefits with observable detail, numbers, timeframes, or
   real-life context where the evidence supports them. (pages 24 and 26)
3. **Filter for the winning zone.** Prefer the overlap between what customers
   want and what the brand does best, while checking what competitors already
   do best. The diagram treats the three-way comparison as a selection tool,
   not permission to claim uniqueness without proof. (pages 27–31)
4. **Find the emotional consequence in customer evidence.** Use interviews,
   surveys, focus groups, and reviews to compare feelings before and after use.
   Keep only emotions that follow naturally from the functional outcome; the
   workshop explicitly rejects unrelated pairings. (pages 44–48)
5. **Test the identity consequence.** Ask whether the product helps express
   individuality, social standing, accomplishment, values, or cultural
   affiliation. An emotional benefit describes a feeling; a self-expressive
   benefit describes who the customer sees themselves as being. (pages 50–52
   and 55–61)

The workshop's five statement templates are variations on the same slots:
**brand + category + target audience + pain or status quo + distinctive benefit
or outcome + credible differentiator**. “Without [pain]” is optional; the reason
to believe is not. Use the shortest arrangement that preserves those slots
rather than copying a template mechanically. (pages 63–67)

**Alignment check.** The deck frames purpose and expression from the company
side and positioning from the customer side. A value proposition that the
identity cannot deliver creates brand disconnect; an identity with no fitting
value promise leaves potential untapped. Check that the promise, experience,
voice, and visual expression tell the same story. (pages 4–8)

**Evidence boundary.** The deck also contains numerical performance claims on
pages 38 and 53. Its references are not complete enough to use those figures as
proof in client copy. Treat them as research leads to verify independently, not
as baked-in evidence.

## 3. Messaging is not one artifact

An important structural point that most copy advice misses:

> "the messaging you equip your marketing team with for one-to-many connections
> with customers at scale may not be the same lens of messaging that you equip
> your sales team with... you might have niche messaging surrounded by a vertical
> or a job to be done for marketing, but a very specific solution messaging
> framework for sales. That's very common."
> (*06-Messaging > 06-Enable empower and evangelize* @ 25:10)

The unifying constraint: "even though it may be different, it's all related under
that same narrative, that same company story" (@ 25:10).

**And the instruction on friction:** "Lean into the friction and understand that
that's okay" (@ 25:10). Teams disagreeing about messaging is a signal the lenses
differ legitimately, not that someone is wrong.

## 4. Finding the differentiated position

### Establish the demand type first

Positioning changes shape depending on what the buyer already has to compare you
against — "depending on your customer's current reference points, you're going to
position yourself differently." The course takes the three-way split from
SiriusDecisions (*12-Positioning > 05-How to Identify Your Demand Type* @ 00:00):

| Demand type | What it is | What the copy has to do |
|---|---|---|
| **New concept** | Truly innovative, no reference points | Explain what it does and why they should care — and expect **no budget allocated** for it |
| **New paradigm** | Retools an existing process — "your longer-lasting light bulb" | They know the job; you argue the better way |
| **Established category** | "Your red oceans" | Differentiate against known alternatives |

Worked examples given: Engagio pioneering account-based marketing tooling when
the tactic itself was unfamiliar (new concept); and the SaaS staple of "people do
this on spreadsheets and we've built a tool to get you off spreadsheets" (new
paradigm) (@ 02:20).

The most actionable line is the budget one. A new-concept product is not
competing for a line item that exists — which is a positioning problem before it
is a copy problem, and explains why category-education content outperforms
feature copy at that stage.

A nine-question set is provided to determine which you are in, recorded on the
positioning story worksheet (@ 07:41).

*Transcription note: the transcript renders the consultancy as "Serious
Decisions" and the vendor as "Engageo". These are **SiriusDecisions** and
**Engagio**.*

### The Monster exercise

From *13-Radical Differentiation > 08-The Monster* @ 03:52. The exercise is a
list, and the questions are worth running verbatim:

- What causes your customers pain? What do you believe is causing them pain?
- What frustrates them? What gets in the way of their progress, their purpose?
- What is your **minimum viable market** most sick of hearing?
- **What are the clichés that they hate?**
- What alternatives were they using?

The clichés question is the sharpest of these and the most neglected. It gives
you the negative space — the things you must not say — which is usually a faster
route to a distinct voice than trying to invent a positive claim.

The lesson's cautionary example is an industry where "all the music sound the
same" because everyone copies the prevailing style, producing "calls around
personality instead of focusing on the art" (@ 03:52). Differentiation decays by
imitation; it needs re-establishing, not just discovering once.

### Where the term comes from, and what it costs

Radical differentiation is credited to **Marty Neumeier**, in his book **ZAG**.
The course's own definition:

> "A business strategy disguised as a positioning method to make your brand the
> only choice in a specific category for a specific group of people."

It is aimed at brands wanting to grow share in saturated markets or become number
one in a category. The instructor then attaches an unusually blunt condition:

> "But — and this is a big but — it is not. It requires a lot of sacrifice. It
> requires a long-term commitment across the board, across the entire
> experience."

(*13-Radical Differentiation > 02-Intro to Radical Differentiation* @ 02:23)

Two further notes from the same lesson: the work is iterative — "ship, get
feedback, see how it works and do it all over again" — and when it is right the
inputs from customers, intuition, and customer-facing staff "click together" like
Lego bricks. If the pieces are not clicking, the position is not found yet.

*Transcription note: the audio renders the author as "Martin Umeyer". It is
**Marty Neumeier**, author of* ZAG.

### Attribute differentiation must be ownable

The added interview distinguishes message-level resonance from factual
attribute differentiation. First listen for language that repeatedly "clicks"
with the market; then investigate the competitors and what the product actually
does differently (*13-Radical Differentiation >
09-everyone-hates-marketers__make-your-brand-stand-out* @ 32:54–34:25).

An attribute is not a position merely because it can be claimed. The standard
given is whether the target audience cares about it and whether the business can
own it by being substantially—described as roughly 10×—better than the next
option (@ 35:16). If neither condition holds, the attribute belongs in feature
copy, not in the positioning.

## 5. Translating positioning into a sales play

The handoff most teams fumble. Sequence as taught
(*04-Positioning  Company Storytelling > 08-Translate your positioning into a
sales play* @ 00:00):

1. **Start with an authentic insight** — the shift in the market that created the
   opportunity for your business.
2. **Align on a shared view of that impact** — explicitly "about you and your
   customer agreeing," e.g. on-premise to cloud, monolithic to microservices,
   "and it has a direct impact on your team and the organization as a whole."

Note what step 1 is *not*: a claim about your product. It is a claim about the
world that the reader can verify independently. That is what makes step 2 —
agreement — possible. A page that opens with product capability has skipped
straight to a conclusion the reader has no reason to accept.

## 6. Story as the delivery vehicle

Positioning arrives as narrative, not as a statement. Two supports in the corpus:

**The four elements of a brand story** are taught as their own lesson, alongside
why the story matters at all (*04-Positioning  Company Storytelling >
02-Stories Convey Meaning* @ 01:54).

**Design thinking applied to storytelling** — "a solution based model... not just
for designers": empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test. The instruction that
matters is the last two: "develop story concepts or prototypes to test in your
market" (*08-Storytelling > 03-Introduction to Design Thinking for storytelling*
@ 06:27).

Positioning is a hypothesis. Prototype and test it rather than committing a
launch to an untested premise.

### The promised land — what the story is actually for

The destination the narrative walks toward: "the picture that you need to paint
for your customers, that after they engage with you, their life is gonna be
better in some way." The instructor's observation is that strong companies "tell
the promised land story in a variety of ways" across their marketing rather than
fixing it in one statement (*12-Positioning > 07-How to Craft an Effective
Promised Land* @ 00:46).

The lesson says there are five elements and enumerates these four before moving
on to proof:

1. **They thrive in the new world** — the one your change-and-stakes section
   established.
2. **It is difficult to reach without you.** "The promised land shouldn't be
   something they can easily do on their own."
3. **The villain is defeated** — they move past the frustration and the pain.
4. **They become a better version of themselves** — "they self-actualize and they
   belong."

Element three is why the villain matters mechanically rather than
dramatically: with no villain there is nothing for the promised land to be free
of. Element four is the one most B2B copy omits entirely.

**Then, and only then, the features.** Superpowers are selected "as part of your
narrative that shows the how, and carves you out as the only person capable of
helping them reap the promised land." The ordering is stated explicitly against
the traditional approach, where "features and benefits, your product, led the
way" (@ 02:42):

> "The story is the point of differentiation. Your simple promise is what they
> remember. Features are just the support of that." (@ 03:00)

That sentence is the cleanest statement in the corpus of why `frameworks.md`'s
feature-benefit advice sits *downstream* of this file.

## 7. Branding as deliberate association

Hormozi's branding playbook is useful here as an execution layer, not as a
replacement for positioning. It defines a brand as the accumulated expectation
and memory a buyer attaches to an option, then gives a practical sequence:

1. Identify the ideal customer.
2. List the things that customer already likes, values, or wants to be seen
   with.
3. Pair the product with those associations deliberately.
4. Deliver the promised experience so the association is reinforced rather than
   contradicted.

(Alex Hormozi — *$100M Playbook: Branding*, pages 8, 19, and 22.)

The copy implication is specific: do not add a “premium” adjective and call it
branding. Choose the audience, choose the association, and show the experience
that earns it. The playbook argues that positive associations can support a
premium relative to an otherwise generic product (page 20), while also warning
that a bad delivery creates a negative association and suppresses repeat buying
(page 27). That makes the promise-and-experience check part of positioning, not
an afterthought for the brand team.

**Evidence boundary.** The playbook includes illustrative return and price
examples (page 21). Keep the mechanism as a hypothesis to test; do not reuse
its numerical outcomes as client proof without independent evidence.

## 8. Working sequence

1. **Name the status quo.** What is the reader doing today, including nothing?
   (§1)
2. **Run the pain and cliché list.** Especially what your minimum viable market is
   sick of hearing. (§4)
3. **Find the authentic insight** — the market shift, stated so the reader can
   agree with it before you mention yourself. (§5)
4. **Settle positioning before value proposition before messaging.** (§2)
5. **Build the benefit ladder and filter it through the winning zone.** (§2)
6. **Build the lenses you actually need** — marketing and sales may differ, under
   one narrative. (§3)
7. **Prototype the story and test it.** (§6)
8. **Only then write the page.** `frameworks.md` for structure, `headlines.md`
   for the opening.

## 9. New-source additions: position from the buyer's frame

### Competitive alternatives → attributes → value → who cares

April Dunford's positioning canvas makes the comparison explicit. Start with
the alternatives a customer would actually use if the product did not exist.
Then map unique attributes to the value those attributes enable, identify who
cares most about that value, and only then choose the market frame that makes
the value easy to understand. A feature is not a differentiator in isolation;
it is one only against a real alternative. Value is the customer's goal, not a
restatement of the feature. (*Obviously Awesome*, chapters Two, Eight, Nine,
Ten, and Twelve.)

**Copy-lab application:** add these five fields to every positioning brief:
alternative, unique attribute, customer value, “who cares a lot,” and category
frame. If one field is blank, the promise is still a hypothesis.

### Jobs are progress in a circumstance

Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan define a Job to Be Done as the progress a
customer wants in a particular circumstance, including functional, emotional,
and social dimensions. Their practical warning is to distinguish the “Big
Hire” (purchase) from the repeated “Little Hires” (use), and to ask what the
buyer must fire before hiring the new solution. A job is better written as
verb + noun than as an adjective such as “convenience.” (*Competing Against
Luck*, chapters Two, Five, Six, Seven, and Ten.)

This changes the copy brief: record the triggering circumstance, the old
workaround, the desired progress, the tradeoff, and the evidence of repeated
use. Do not infer a job from a demographic label alone.

### Discovery and reach are constraints, not decoration

Teresa Torres's opportunity framing says not to chase every pain point: map the
opportunity space, then prioritize opportunities that can move the desired
business outcome. Byron Sharp's category evidence is a check against assuming
that a brand's current demographic skew is its natural audience; investigate
whether the skew reflects reach or marketing history before narrowing the
message. (*Continuous Discovery Habits*, chapter “Continuous Discovery Habits”;
*How Brands Grow*, page 75.)

**Guardrail:** these are strategic lenses, not permission to claim a market
segment or performance result without customer evidence.
