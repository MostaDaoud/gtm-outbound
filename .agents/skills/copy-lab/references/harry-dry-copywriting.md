# Harry Dry — practical copy review and rewriting

Use this reference for ads, landing pages, headlines, positioning lines,
newsletters, and anti-generic edits. It distills Harry Dry's working methods
from the user-supplied captions for *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*.

This is a practice guide, not a universal style law. Apply each test in the
reader's actual channel, preserve verified claims, and do not invent facts to
make a sentence more concrete.

---

## 1. The three-question sentence test

Review an important sentence with three questions:

1. **Can the reader visualize it?** Replace an abstract benefit with a person,
   object, action, place, or observable before/after.
2. **Can the claim be proved true or false?** Point to evidence instead of
   leaning on praise words.
3. **Could only this product plausibly say it?** Reject lines a competitor
   could sign unchanged.

Dry introduces all three at the start, then contrasts an interchangeable
recruitment slogan with New Balance's specific pairing of supermodels in London
and dads in Ohio. The latter is picturable, checkable, and tied to the brand's
actual audience span. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 01:30–02:30)

### Operational scorecard

For each load-bearing line, mark:

- `V` — visual or concrete
- `F` — falsifiable and supported
- `O` — ownable by this product

`VFO` is a strong line. Missing one letter is a revision prompt, not an
automatic failure. Legal copy, navigation, and necessary category labels may
need clarity more than novelty.

### Example transformation

Weak: `Regain your fitness.`

Zoom in:

- Regain from what? Sitting on the couch.
- Fitness of what kind? Running.
- What observable finish line? 5K.

Result: `From couch to 5K.`

Dry uses this progression to show how repeated “what do I actually mean?”
questions turn an abstraction into a visible journey. (Harry Dry — *Learn
Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 04:00–05:30)

## 2. Zoom in until the noun or action appears

When a phrase feels polished but empty:

1. Write the abstract phrase at the top of a page.
2. Ask what it means in this product and reader's life.
3. Rewrite it one level more specifically.
4. Repeat until a reader can picture an action, object, person, place, or
   measurable state.

The useful distinction is concrete versus abstract, not informal versus
formal. “Better way,” “seamless transition,” and “change an industry” are hard
to see. A charging pit bull, a specific commute behavior, or a named product
detail gives memory something to hold. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76
Minutes*, @ 03:00–04:30)

Do not manufacture sensory detail. If the brief does not contain the required
specific, leave `[SPECIFIC NEEDED: …]` and ask for it.

## 3. Point, do not merely praise

To make a claim falsifiable, replace the adjective with what caused the
judgment:

| Praise word | Point to |
|-------------|----------|
| intelligent | a relevant action, result, or body of work |
| reliable | uptime, delivery history, failure rate, or guarantee |
| easy | steps, elapsed time, required inputs, or a live demonstration |
| valuable | money earned, time saved, errors avoided, or a customer outcome |
| popular | verified adoption, retention, repeat purchase, or named users |

Dry's shorthand is to imagine that you cannot describe; you may only point.
His gold example moves from “great investment” to a chart and relevant market
behavior. The work is research: locate the thing that makes the judgment
reasonable. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 06:30–09:00)

### Fact-to-copy method

1. Collect facts before drafting.
2. Select one that contains tension, surprise, contrast, or consequence.
3. Ask what story or argument the fact implies.
4. Preserve the fact's exact scope and source.
5. Build the copy around it; do not bury it beneath adjectives.

A fact is a starting point, not automatically a headline. Dry explicitly notes
that a true number may still be dull; the writer has to find what it reveals.
His principle is that facts ground the story and give language precision.
(Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 45:30–49:30)

## 4. Make the line ownable

Apply the competitor-sign test:

> Could a direct competitor put its logo under this line without changing a
> word?

If yes, inspect:

- a product behavior competitors do not share;
- an unusual customer mix or use case;
- a constraint the company chose deliberately;
- a verified comparison;
- a belief the company acts on;
- a category enemy or old way the product replaces.

Dry's Volvo example uses a visible instrument detail to create a claim tied to
that car. His larger rule is to find something no one else is doing that the
audience actually cares about; mere difference is insufficient. (Harry Dry —
*Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 09:00–10:00 and @ 12:00–13:30)

Comparative claims still require evidence and legal review where applicable.
Ownable does not mean exaggerated.

## 5. Start with the attitude shift

Before choosing AIDA or another page structure, define:

- **Point A:** What does this reader currently believe, know, fear, or ignore?
- **Point B:** What must they believe or understand to take the next action?

The copy is the wire between those two poles. Dry then divides the job into:

1. **Who are you talking to?** Segment, awareness, situation, and attention.
2. **What do you have to say?** A fact, belief, argument, or useful difference.
3. **How will you say it well?** The wording, rhythm, image, and structure.

(Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 21:00–22:00 and @ 25:00–29:00)

This order matters. Wordplay cannot rescue the wrong audience or the absence of
an idea.

## 6. Test the copy where the reader will encounter it

The writing environment should reproduce the reading environment:

- Put a billboard among other billboards and street clutter.
- Draft the email in its real width and type scale.
- Review mobile landing-page folds, not only a clean desktop canvas.
- Judge an ad with its image and visual hierarchy.
- Check the line among competing feed posts, not alone on a blank document.

Dry writes directly in the delivery tool because copy and design change one
another. A line that works by itself may wrap badly, disappear beside competing
elements, or take too long to decode in context. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting
in 76 Minutes*, @ 24:00–25:00 and @ 35:30–36:30)

### The quick-comprehension check

For an attention-constrained ad, show it in context for roughly two seconds,
hide it, then ask:

- What was it selling?
- What was the point?
- What remains in memory?

Dry calls this the “one Mississippi, two Mississippi” test. It is most useful
for ads and first-screen communication, not for every sentence in long-form
copy. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 14:00–15:00)

## 7. Use contrast to make the argument visible

When the central point feels shapeless, divide a page and list opposites. Dry
groups useful conflict into:

- **Approaches:** two ways to solve the same problem;
- **Beliefs:** what the audience or brand accepts versus rejects;
- **Competitors:** two products or categories in direct contrast.

Before/after and problem/solution also work. The point is not aggression; the
contrast helps the reader understand each side relatively. (Harry Dry — *Learn
Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 29:30–31:30 and @ 52:00–53:30)

For stories, look for the implicit **but**: what changed, resisted, failed, or
complicated the expected path? Do not mechanically insert the word in every
paragraph. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 64:00–65:30)

## 8. Build, rewrite, and compare versions

Dry's demonstrated ad did not arrive as a polished first draft. His sequence:

1. Save a promising seed — often a fact or argument.
2. Add a contrasting alternative or enemy.
3. Borrow a relevant layout principle from a strong example.
4. Draft directly with the design.
5. Rewrite headings and body for clarity, rhythm, hierarchy, and parallelism.
6. Generate weak versions without judging them too early; let the “dirty
   water” run before selecting.
7. Show trusted reviewers multiple alternatives so their feedback can compare
   real choices.
8. Keep refining until each element earns its place.

The example took about 25 rewrites and developed piece by piece. This supports
iteration, not a mandatory rewrite count. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76
Minutes*, @ 29:00–41:00)

### Simplicity is an editing result

Dry treats simple writing as rewriting. Apply this deletion test:

- Is this word doing work?
- Does this sentence make the paragraph stronger?
- If the sentence is removed, does the paragraph lose its logic or force?
- Is one idea carrying the section, or has `and` joined unrelated promises?

His cited Kaplan rule is that words not working for the sentence work against
it. He extends the same idea to scope: a narrower idea is often stronger.
(Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 56:00–59:00)

When seeking feedback, present two or three controlled versions of the same
unit. Reviewers can combine the best line with the best structure more usefully
than they can respond to one isolated draft. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in
76 Minutes*, @ 59:00–60:00)

## 9. Structure with dividing lines and parallelism

To structure a large subject:

1. Find a meaningful dividing line.
2. Turn the sides into parallel labels.
3. Order them so each answers a distinct reader question.

Dry reduces copywriting to audience, substance, and expression; he splits an
audience-growth essay into the first 100 readers and the next 9,900. The labels
make a large problem graspable without pretending every topic has three equal
parts. (Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 60:00–62:00)

Parallelism should sharpen meaning. Do not alter a verified number solely to
make lines symmetrical.

## 10. Newsletter application

Dry distinguishes a newsletter from a generic blog post by emphasizing the
letter:

- Open with time, place, and what is happening now.
- Use observed detail that could not come from a generic feed.
- Deliver a dense set of useful examples.
- Use contrast inside examples so the lesson is easier to understand.
- Keep paragraphs easy to enter and move through on the reading device.

(Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 50:30–54:30)

His own two-line paragraph ceiling is explicitly described as idiosyncratic,
not universally good. Preserve a client's established rhythm and use short
paragraphs because the channel and thought require them, not to imitate a
surface mannerism.

## 11. AI-assisted writing: tool plus human judgment

Dry frames AI as a tool whose usefulness depends on three human inputs:

- **Taste:** knowing which fact, line, crop, or example deserves emphasis;
- **Conviction:** believing the argument strongly enough to choose and defend
  it;
- **Experience:** noticing real behavior and having material that was not
  generated from stock language.

(Harry Dry — *Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes*, @ 65:30–69:30)

Copy Lab implementation:

1. Use models for retrieval, synthesis, alternatives, and critique.
2. Anchor the draft in verified research, customer language, and first-hand
   material.
3. Make the human choice explicit: which argument matters, which evidence is
   sufficient, and which line fits the brand.
4. Never fabricate experience or “human” quirks.
5. Apply the VFO test and anti-generic pass before delivery.

This complements `ai-workflow.md` and `anti-generic-copy.md`; it does not make
authorship claims or promise detector outcomes.

## 12. Final Harry Dry pass

- [ ] The reader's current and desired attitudes are named.
- [ ] The audience, reading situation, and attention level are explicit.
- [ ] The main line is visual, supported, and ownable where feasible.
- [ ] Abstract benefits have been zoomed into observable specifics.
- [ ] Praise words point to evidence.
- [ ] A fact, belief, or argument gives the copy something to say.
- [ ] The central contrast is understandable without manufactured hostility.
- [ ] Copy and design were reviewed in the final channel context.
- [ ] The first screen passes the appropriate comprehension test.
- [ ] Several versions were compared for important headlines and CTAs.
- [ ] Every remaining word and idea contributes.
- [ ] AI-assisted language is grounded in human taste, conviction, and real
      experience.
- [ ] No fact, comparison, quote, or personal detail was invented.
