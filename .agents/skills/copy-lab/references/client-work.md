# Client Work

Running the engagement: what to test, what to advise, and where to draw lines.
Every claim carries its lesson and timestamp.

---

## 1. The ethics conversation is part of the job

The most instructive client story in the corpus. Schutz's team found a technique
producing a 10–15% uplift, and the client declined it:

> "our customer said 'I don't care about that 10 to 15% uplift, I just don't want
> to bother people with problems like that, doesn't feel good, I'm not gonna do
> it.' And **I like clients that do that** — appreciate the knowledge, appreciate
> the test, consider their behavioral consequences and take a decision based on
> that."
> (*4. Need & Motivations > 4.5 Techniques* @ 07:15)

He frames the underlying questions plainly (@ 07:15): is it done with good
intention? Is it in line with what the consumer wants? Is society thinking about
it correctly — in other words, **is it ethical to apply it**?

**The professional model this implies:** run the test, report the number, name the
behavioural consequence, and let the client decide. Withholding the finding is
not integrity; presenting it without its cost is not either.

Schutz applies this to himself — he refuses to demonstrate base-rate neglect,
calling it "pure manipulation," and says he tells clients no
(*1.4 System 2 Persuasion Techniques* @ 18:07). Note the asymmetry: he will
decline to *build* something while still teaching that it exists, so practitioners
can recognise it.

## 2. Know which techniques you would refuse before a client asks

Base-rate neglect is the corpus's worked example of a technique that works and
should still be declined. The mechanism: people cannot hold the denominator in
mind. "If you're telling your visitors that 98% of the people rating the product
is positive, people go 'wow, 98% is positive' — whereas maybe only very few people
rate the product" (*1.4* @ 16:25).

Deciding your line in advance turns an awkward negotiation into a stated policy.
It is also easier to charge for judgement you can articulate.

## 3. Test results need alternative explanations before they become advice

The "1267 people bought this" case: the lift came from the *number*, not the
social proof, and disappeared when replaced with "most popular." Schutz's rule:
"if we really want to learn about the customer behavior we should challenge those
alternative explanations" (*8.3 Reducing the pain of paying* @ 29:41).

**Client-facing consequence.** There are two deliverables from any test — the
*result* and the *explanation*. The result is usually solid; the explanation is a
hypothesis. Selling a shaky explanation as a principle is how agencies build
roadmaps on sand.

## 4. Segment before reporting

The telco case: a page about switching **reduced** conversion for prospects and
**increased** subscription renewal for existing customers (*2.1 Characteristics of
System 1* @ 42:11).

An aggregate report on that test would have been wrong in both directions. Before
presenting any result, ask which segments it was computed across — new versus
returning at minimum. See `sales-pages.md` §4.

## 5. Personalisation: feed situation, not personality

On profiling tools: "it doesn't mean it's not useful to use a profiling tool,
because the algorithms are actually pretty cool. It's just that you have to feed
the data... not only with a cookie or a login type of behavior... but also
feeding **contextual data**. And you'll be amazed how many effects you're going to
find that influence your users' behavior way more than their individual
differences" (*6. Self, Social & Situation > 6.3 Techniques* @ 08:08).

He also flags the practical ceiling honestly: acting on it one-to-one is "a hard
thing" (@ 08:08). Useful for setting client expectations — the insight is
reachable, the full personalisation often is not.

Underlying evidence in `voice.md` §7 and `research.md` §2.

## 6. What testing actually looks like on live accounts

Documented engagements worth citing to clients as precedent:

- **Loans** — emphasising the end goal (new kitchen, new car) and removing the
  words "credit" and "loan" raised conversion across all tests
  (*4.5 Techniques* @ 37:57).
- **Savings account** — emphasising a single interest rate beat full redesigns
  from two competing agencies (*1.4* @ 13:31).
- **Hotel** — the same eight USPs, merely chunked into three layered groups, won
  (*1.4* @ 15:13).
- **Cookie consent** — a forced-choice lightbox took opt-in from ~20% to 75%
  (*1.4* @ 04:47).
- **Conrad** — *reducing* the number of filters won (*1.4* @ 26:09).

The pattern across all five: the winning change was **subtractive or
re-emphasising**, not additive. Useful when a client wants a redesign and the
evidence says re-prioritise.

## 7. Reporting in the client's own frame

From the enablement module: isolating revenue impact of one programme is "very
difficult... so many things are changing in the context of a business — product
configurations, product offerings, pricing." The recommended response is to
report in the funnel shape the company already uses, so the conversation is about
"what's happening, what's working, and where are there areas to improve"
(*Product Marketing, 10-Sales and Customer Success Enablement >
02-Measuring Enablement* @ 01:47).

Claiming a copy change caused a revenue figure is usually indefensible and invites
the one challenge you cannot win. Reporting stage-level movement in their existing
reporting frame is both more honest and more durable.

## 8. The handover is not the finish line

The enablement module is blunt that training which does not change behaviour was
wasted. Coaching is placed deliberately last in the sequence, and the reason is
given plainly:

> "Call coaching is the final step of that process, because each of the first
> steps do not matter if it's not translating into a change in behavior in
> customer-facing situations."

(*10-Sales and Customer Success Enablement > 06-Coaching Enablement* @ 06:38)

Two mechanisms are offered. **Assess consistently**, so the standard scales
rather than depending on who is watching; and **record and score the actual
conversations**, which is what "closes the loop on the holistic enablement
effort."

**The diagnostic question, worth stealing verbatim:**

> "Are your managers consistently reinforcing the reps' training? How do you
> know?"

If the answer to the second half is "we don't," that is the gap — not the
training. The lesson's assignment is to walk every activity you ask a team to
perform and check whether their managers were ever told **how to coach to the
desired behaviour** (@ 06:38).

**Why this belongs in a copywriter's file.** A copy deck, a voice guide or a
messaging framework has exactly the same failure mode: it is adopted for a
fortnight and then quietly abandoned because nobody owns reinforcing it. Ask at
proposal stage who will be reinforcing the change after you leave, and price the
answer in. A framework nobody maintains reverts to the category voice — which is
the decay described in `positioning.md` §4.

## 9. Engagement checklist

1. **Is the technique one you would defend publicly?** (§1, §2)
2. **Have you separated the result from the explanation?** (§3)
3. **Which segments was this computed across?** (§4)
4. **Are personalisation promises grounded in situation data?** (§5)
5. **Is the recommendation subtractive where evidence supports it?** (§6)
6. **Does the report use the client's own funnel language?** (§7)
7. **Has the client been given the behavioural cost alongside the uplift?** (§1)
8. **Who reinforces this after you leave, and do they know how?** (§8)
