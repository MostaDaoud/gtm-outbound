# Headlines & Hooks

Getting the first sentence read, and the second. Every claim carries its lesson
and timestamp.

The corpus's headline advice is unusually mechanistic: it explains *why* an
opening works in terms of attention and curiosity, rather than offering a swipe
list of templates. That makes it portable to formats the templates never covered.

---

## 1. Curiosity is the strongest lever

Schutz includes it with visible reluctance and then endorses it hard: "I doubted
whether I would include it in the course but I think it's too powerful not to —
it's curiosity. If you bring something into a user's conscious awareness that
makes him curious that's very powerful in where he puts his attention"
(*3. Attention & Perception > 3.3 Attention* @ 11:10).

**The worked test in that lesson is the whole lesson.** Brian Massey's AdWords
test pitted a conventional authority claim — roughly *we hold all the secrets to
internet marketing* — against:

> "lousy marketing and dated IDs — don't come to our site"

The second was "a big winner" (@ 11:10).

Read what that headline does: it disqualifies the reader, contradicts the
expected sales posture, and leaves a gap the reader has to close. Confidence
claims close the gap; curiosity headlines open one.

---

## 2. Surprise, front and back

From Schutz's five tips for a persuasive offer narrative: "surprise, because
surprises are the changes — remember Kahneman. So especially in the beginning and
in the end surprise people, because if you surprise in the first sentence they'll
get their attention" (*7. Persuasive Marketing > 7.2 Techniques for Your Offer*
@ 16:50).

The mechanism he gives is more useful than the instruction. A surprising opening
**sets a narrative goal** — it creates a problem the reader now needs to resolve,
which "boosts the need for them to find the meaning behind it, to find the
solution." The payoff: "more likeable, more memorable and more trustworthy"
(@ 16:50).

So a headline is not a summary. A headline that summarises the article removes
the reason to read it.

**Position matters:** beginning *and* end. The final line of a section is the
second-best place to spend a surprise, because it carries the reader into the
next one.

## 3. The story-shaped headline

The most-quoted headline in advertising is cited in the corpus for a specific
reason — it is a story in one line:

> "They laughed when I sat down to play"
> (*Digital Psychology & Persuasion, 18-Cognitive Biases > 11-Narrative Fallacy*
> @ 00:38, paraphrased on the recording)

The lesson's framing: "Our brains love stories and so if you can put your
information in the context of a story it will be more effective and more
memorable" (@ 00:38).

That headline contains a character, a conflict, an implied reversal, and an
unresolved ending — in seven words. Test your headline for those elements before
reaching for adjectives.

**The warning attached to the same lesson.** The narrative fallacy is that
successful people "are more or less crafting a narrative to connect the dots
rather than really giving you a formula" (@ 00:38). Stories are persuasive
*because* they bypass scrutiny. Use the mechanism; do not fool yourself with it
when reading your own case studies.

## 4. Voice-led headlines

Oatly's "It's like milk but made for humans" is cited as a deliberate refusal of
the safe option for the audience (*Branding & Brand Strategy, 14-Branding >
05-Build a brand voice* @ 01:28). The body copy continues in the same register:
"so what is this oat drink anyway? Milk? No, it's not milk."

The point being made is not that humour works. It is that the headline is
*legible as that brand* before you see the logo. If your headline would sit
equally well on a competitor's page, it is doing positioning work for the
category rather than for you. See `positioning.md` §4 on clichés.

## 5. Fear-based openings — two conditions

If your hook raises a threat, Schutz's constraint is that the threat alone
backfires without two supports (*2. System 1 > 2.4 Persuasion Techniques*
@ 11:06):

1. **Response efficacy** — the reader must believe the action removes the threat:
   "is this gonna lead to the effect that I wanted to."
2. **Self-efficacy** — "the moment you are inducing fear they have to feel that
   they can do it."

His example is people who are fully aware of severe health threats and do not
change, because the second condition is missing (@ 11:06). Awareness was never
the bottleneck.

**Practical rule:** never let a fear headline stand more than a screen away from
evidence that the fix works *and* is achievable by this reader.

## 6. Where the headline sits in the page

Morrison places the opening's job precisely: "to attract and grab the attention
of your best buyers... pique their interest and enchant them into reading more...
spark curiosity that's going to keep them engaged throughout the entire sales
page" (*Words Are Wands, 06-Bonus > 02-How to Write Sales Pages That Actually
Sell (part 1)* @ 06:17).

Note "your best buyers," not all readers — the Massey headline in §1 works by
excluding people.

Her practical caution from the same lesson: this sentence invites perfectionism,
and writing linearly from it stalls the draft (@ 06:17). Draft the body, then
come back for the headline once you know what the page actually promises.

## 7. Sub-headlines are the version most people read

The persuasive-design module states it as a rule rather than a preference:

> "After every two paragraphs, you need to have a sub-headline. Because the most
> dominant behavior online is **scanning** — and people do that by reading your
> headline and sub-headlines. That's it. If you don't have sub-headlines, you've
> lost. They don't know anything."

(*Digital Psychology & Persuasion, 03-Digital Psychology  Persuasion Minidegree >
02-Principles of Persuasive Design* @ 16:03)

Two consequences, and the second is the one usually missed:

1. **Density.** Two paragraphs is the stated maximum between sub-headings.
2. **The sub-headline layer has to carry the argument on its own.** If a reader
   sees only the headline and the sub-headings, they should still get the case.
   Sub-headings that merely label sections ("Features", "How it works") waste the
   one layer that is reliably read.

This is the structural counterpart to `psychology.md` §1: a scanning reader is a
low-ability reader in the HSM sense, and the sub-headline layer is what you are
actually writing for them.

## 8. Adapt the hook to format and channel

> "Every good storyline has a hook — something that draws the reader in. A hook
> is critical to your storyline and should be **customized based on the type of
> content** you create, whether it's an article, a video, a blog post, **and the
> channels** you plan to use."

(*Product Marketing, 04-Positioning  Company Storytelling > 07-How to craft
effective messaging* @ 01:20)

The same idea therefore needs a different opening per surface. This is the
strategic version of the headline/hook/subject-line split — one narrative, three
entry points, none of them reusable verbatim.

### Homepage value proposition: clarity before cleverness

For broad homepage traffic, the new value-proposition lesson treats the
headline as a roughly ten-word pitch. It must state the product and desired
benefit plainly; an ownable differentiator is useful only if it fits without
obscuring the offer (*Product Marketing, 13-Radical Differentiation >
11-everyone-hates-marketers__value-proposition-what-is* @ 38:01; @ 42:21).

The worked line is: **"Shop the world's largest selection of built-to-last,
weathertight pet doors."** Every component came from research: product/category,
selection as the reason customers chose the seller, and durability plus weather
protection as the outcomes visitors wanted (@ 44:37–45:24). The lesson's warning
is blunt: attempts to be exceptional through subtle wording often hide the fact
that the company has what the visitor came for (@ 44:02).

## 9. Checklist

Before shipping a headline:

1. **Does it open a gap, or close one?** Summaries close. (§1)
2. **Is there a surprise in the first sentence?** (§2)
3. **Can you find a character and a conflict in it?** (§3)
4. **Would it work on a competitor's site?** If yes, rewrite. (§4)
5. **If it raises a fear, is the fix visible and achievable?** (§5)
6. **Is it aimed at your best buyer, even at the cost of excluding others?** (§6)
7. **Was it written after the body?** (§6)
8. **Do the sub-headings alone carry the argument?** (§7)
9. **Is this hook built for the surface it will appear on?** (§8)
10. **On a homepage, is the value proposition clear before it is clever?** (§8)

## 10. New-source additions: promise first, curiosity second

Ogilvy's advertising notes put research behind the promise and treat a
benefit-bearing headline as the gate to the rest of the copy. *Tested
Advertising Methods* adds a useful ranking: self-interest and a clear benefit
usually beat curiosity alone; a curiosity headline has to earn the reader's
time with a believable payoff. The book also recommends writing many candidates
and judging them from the bored reader's point of view. (*Ogilvy on Advertising*,
chapter “Miracles of research”; *Tested Advertising Methods*, pages 11–13,
16, 21–25.)

**New headline pass**

1. Write the clearest benefit promise for the best-fit reader.
2. Add a concrete circumstance, mechanism, or story only if it makes the
   promise more believable or specific.
3. Test curiosity as a wrapper, not as a substitute for the payoff.
4. Generate a batch, then cut any line that could fit a competitor unchanged.

Heath and Heath's SUCCESs checklist is a compact quality screen: simple,
unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and story-shaped. Minto's pyramid
principle supplies the editing order after the hook: lead with the governing
answer, then group supporting reasons, then evidence. (*Made to Stick*, pages
33, 53, 116, 138, 218, and 269; *The Minto Pyramid Principle*.)
