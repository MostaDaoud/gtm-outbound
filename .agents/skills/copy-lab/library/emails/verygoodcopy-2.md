# VeryGoodCopy - Reference, Part 2 of 2

Source: three mailbox exports of the VeryGoodCopy newsletter ("VeryGoodCopy Emails", Parts 3, 4, 5), 285 emails, sent **01 Jul 2020 to 18 Dec 2023**. Roughly 2,600 words of unique essay content per 10 emails; the rest is launch copy, waitlists and footers.

Author: Eddie Shleyner, Chicago. Newsletter about copywriting, creativity and storytelling. Grew from about 10,000 subscribers (Sep 2020) to 63,303 (Dec 2023). Two-time HackerNoon "Email Newsletter of the Year" (claimed Dec 2022 and Dec 2023). The business moved through four phases inside this window: free micro-essays plus a Patreon community called VGC Plus (2020-2021); waitlist then launch of a video course, Transformational Landing Pages or "TLP" (Nov 2022 to Nov 2023); an affiliate bundle with Kevin Rogers' Escape Velocity (May 2023); and a book pre-order (2023).

**Dominant themes**

1. Creativity is connectivity, not originality. Stated in some form in at least nine emails.
2. The reader's imagination does the work. Minimalism, "realism", sparse detail, present tense, invisible dialogue tags.
3. Direct-response fundamentals over tactics: list, offer, creative (40/40/20). Copy is a business tool, not art.
4. Curiosity as the primary engine: fascinations, impossible headlines, open loops.
5. Slowness as method: slow looking, slow writing, gestation, creative rest, monotasking.
6. The human condition as the only durable moat, especially against AI.
7. Personal narrative as the teaching vehicle. Almost every principle arrives inside a story about his wife Kelsey, his son Beau, his daughter Sofia, his grandfather Felix, or a stranger.

**How this era differs from the later material in Part 1**

Part 1 covers the later work. Treat any conflict between the two files as this file being older, and Part 1 being the corrected position. Within this era the markers are: heavy launch and waitlist machinery (roughly 90 of 285 emails are promotional or deadline-driven), a Patreon paywall, A/B-tested subject lines sent as near-duplicate emails minutes apart, and doctrine delivered through borrowed stories (Mad Men, films, documentaries, other copywriters' interviews) rather than through named frameworks. The frameworks he does name here (Bob Stone's Gem, P.A.S., 40/40/20, the CRIT system) are always attributed to someone else. His own contribution is the vignette plus the turn.

## How to cite

Cite as `("Subject line", YYYY-MM-DD)`, using the send date from the email header, not the internal title. Where a subject line was A/B tested, the companion variant is noted. Example: `("Writing in images", 2021-11-03)`.

Two cautions. Subject lines and internal titles frequently disagree; the internal H1 is usually the truer name, so give both when they differ. And the corpus contains deliberate resends of the same issue under different subject lines within minutes, so identical arguments appearing twice usually means one issue tested twice, not two issues.

## Core doctrine

**1. Copy exists to be understood without effort.**
"Words like 'impaction' and 'tooth decay' make the audience do the work of understanding, of imagining. No good. The audience must not work to understand."
("Writing in images", 2021-11-03)

**2. The brain decodes pictures faster than words, so write in images.**
"It's easier for your brain to decode a picture into words than it is to encode words into a picture."
("Writing in images", 2021-11-03)

**3. A benefit is flat until you drill it.**
"What is the benefit of the benefit? And what is the benefit of that benefit?"
("To make your copy feel more real and compelling", 2022-08-30)

**4. Copy starts with the market; art starts with the maker.**
"Great art starts with the maker. Great ads start with the market."
("Copywriting, art, and the strangest DM I've ever received", 2023-12-07)

**5. Copy is a generous act, not a self-expressive one.**
"Copy, on the other hand, is a generous expression: It's a product of the audience, their needs and worldview."
("Copywriting is not art", 2022-06-02)

**6. You channel demand; you do not create it.**
"the most important thing Gene taught me: copywriters don't create demand. We channel existing demand."
(Ken McCarthy on Eugene Schwartz, "Copywriters are sailing", 2022-05-05)

**7. The ad is a window, not a painting.**
"the words in advertising are like the windows in a store. You must be able to look right through them and see the product."
(Eugene Schwartz, "Copywriting, art, and the strangest DM I've ever received", 2023-12-07)

**8. Human nature does not update, so the fundamentals do not expire.**
"Human nature is perpetual. In most respects it is the same today as in the time of Caesar."
(Claude Hopkins, "Ad teardown (trying something new)", 2020-12-16)

**9. Curiosity is the cheapest attention you can buy.**
"Curiosity," wrote Hopkins, "is one of the strongest human incentives."
("Impossible headlines", 2023-05-02)

**10. Claim the commonplace before your competitor does.**
"say something first and you own it."
("Say something first and you own it", 2022-02-01)

**11. Specificity is what makes a promise believable.**
The lesson drawn from Bob Bly's winning Day-Timer headline: specificity plus trigger words like "INSIDE" and "guaranteed" beat a bigger but vaguer promise by 50 percent.
("Why did this headline beat the control by 50%?", 2021-08-23)

**12. Volume is how you find the signal in feedback.**
"Volume, ultimately, is the secret."
("This copy feedback system will give you scars (and fantastic ads)", 2023-02-02)

**13. Quality is manufactured from quantity.**
"Quality is the product of quantity."
("9 writing "rules" (and 57 examples)", 2023-11-22)

**14. Clarity outranks concision.**
"Clarity beats concision" is Rule #4 of the fascination method; length is allowed when length buys understanding.
("How to write a compelling sentence", 2023-11-21)

**15. Reading copy must never feel like solving a puzzle.**
"Reading copy should always feel easy, even effortless."
(""Bad reading" for new copywriters", 2023-04-06)

**16. Write to think, not to express a finished thought.**
"Write to get ideas," said KK, "not to express them."
(Kevin Kelly, "A writing revelation", 2020-11-24)

**17. Always write to one person.**
"Never write for anyone, always write for someone."
("Good copywriting practice", 2022-03-09)

**18. All compelling scenes contain conflict and change.**
"1 > Conflict 2 > Change" is the whole of Blake Snyder's diagnostic, applied here to ads and posts as well as scripts.
("All compelling stories have these things in common", 2023-02-28)

**19. Loss avoidance beats gain seeking.**
"People will jog to pleasure but we'll sprint from pain."
("7-step formula", 2023-06-09)

**20. Ads make promises, and promises are load-bearing.**
"Ads make promises. Promises bring people hope. Don't mess with a person's hope."
("Horrible, horrible copy", 2022-02-08)

## Technique bank

**1. Dimensionalization ("the So What drill").**
Ask "So what?" of every benefit, then ask it of the answer, until you reach a desire the prospect would not say out loud. His worked example: an extra-large art print goes from "it fits your wall" to "it signals taste to people whose opinion you want". Mechanism: each drill adds a dimension of specificity, and specificity reads as truth.
"Every time you ask and answer this question, you've added a dimension to your benefit."
("To make your copy feel more real and compelling", 2022-08-30)

**2. The Cross-Out Test.**
Write the copy, cross out every mention of your company and product, insert a competitor's name. If the copy still fits, rewrite until it does not. Mechanism: forces a differentiated narrative rather than a category description.
"If the copy still fits, you've a problem"
("Copywriting "Cross-Out Test"", 2021-10-28)

**3. The 40/40/20 audit.**
Score any campaign before and after the fact: 40 percent list, 40 percent offer, 20 percent creative. Mechanism: stops you rewriting copy when the offer or list is the failure.
"nine out of ten times, it had nothing to do with the copy"
(Kim Krause Schwalm, "How to predict the success of an ad campaign", 2023-05-08)

**4. The CRIT system.**
Distribute the draft to everyone in the building, ask each to rip it apart, and read for overlap rather than individual notes. Mechanism: consensus themes are the real defects; outliers are noise.
"It was a system by which the writer would distribute his copy to everybody working in the ad agency"
(Gary Bencivenga, "This copy feedback system will give you scars", 2023-02-02)

**5. Terse red-ink marginalia.**
Bencivenga's readers were encouraged to write short, brutal notes rather than considered critique.
"I don't believe this claim!" / "Get to the point…" / "I'm confused." / "So boring…" / "But why?"
(same source, 2023-02-02)

**6. Put a condition on the claim.**
Qualify a promise with "may", "can", "if", so the reader's scepticism alarm does not fire. Mechanism: belief precedes action, and an unqualified claim reads as hype.
"When you make a claim, put a condition on it."
("This may explode your copy's response", 2022-04-28)

**7. The IF...THEN sandwich.**
Name a clear, simple requirement (IF), then a strong promise (THEN). Deployable as headline, subhead, subject line or tagline.
"cite a clear and simple requirement (IF), and follow it up with a strong promise (THEN)"
(same source, 2022-04-28)

**8. Bob Stone's Gem (7 steps).**
Benefit, expansion, positive, proof, negative, summary, action. Stone's Kiplinger letter ran unbeaten for over 35 years.
"1/ Benefit 2/ Expansion 3/ Positive 4/ Proof 5/ Negative 6/ Summary 7/ Action"
("7-step formula", 2023-06-09)

**9. P.A.S. as a starter motor.**
Problem, agitate with an anecdote the reader can see herself in, solution. Used for articles, landing pages, emails, banners, radio.
"Ever struggle to start writing copy?"
("A simple, versatile, proven copywriting formula", 2022-04-21)

**10. In Medias Res.**
Open inside the crisis, not before it. Mechanism: creates tension and forces the questions "how?" and "why?", which is curiosity by construction.
"In Medias Res drops the audience into a problem, a climactic moment, an argument or a fight"
("Instant engagement technique", 2022-04-12)

**11. Future Pacing.**
Walk the reader through life after the purchase. Trigger words: "will", "imagine", "picture this".
"demonstrate what life will be like with, or without, a product"
("Did this copywriting technique win the presidency?", 2021-12-09)

**12. Preemptive claims.**
Claim a universal practice as if it were proprietary. Hopkins did it for Schlitz with "Every Bottle Sterilized" and "Bottled Purity"; every brewer sterilised, only Schlitz said so.
"say something first and you own it"
("Say something first and you own it", 2022-02-01)

**13. LISH: Length Implies Strength Heuristic.**
More words, more testimonials, more pages read as more substance. Applied as a wall of testimonials on a landing page; he reports his own conversions rose, and cites Justin Welsh moving from "low-to-mid 1 percent up to about 3.6 percent".
"the assumption that more words = more substance"
("This could triple your conversion rate", 2022-10-14)

**14. The zero-ending.**
Stop at the moment of realisation, before the moral is stated, so the reader completes the story herself and carries it out of the page.
"He often ends his stories without a neat, satisfying conclusion."
("The remarkable "zero-ending" storytelling technique", 2022-08-02)

**15. The three rules of "real" writing.**
(1) Never enter a character's head. (2) Withhold detail. (3) Present tense. Mechanism: holes in the text become the reader's own memories.
"Never let The Reader inside the mind of a character"
("The rules of "real" writing", 2023-10-31)

**16. Copywork by hand, slowly, more than once.**
His first editor's prescription: transcribe Bencivenga, Halbert and Caples by hand, twice. Mechanism: you cannot skim what your hand is writing, so syntax and cadence transfer.
"Transcribing another writer's work is called "copyworking""
("Do this to develop your copywriting voice", 2021-06-07)

**17. The Wikipedia concision drill.**
Cut an already-tight Wikipedia paragraph to a third without losing meaning. Daily, five days a week. Mechanism: Wikipedia's own house style already bans fluff, so the exercise isolates syntax.
"Rewrite a Wikipedia paragraph to a third of its original length"
("One of the best copywriting exercises", 2021-06-28)

**18. The 4X then halve process.**
Commit to a final word count, write four times that, cut in half, cut in half again, deciding sentence by sentence with three questions: does this say it faster, is this word necessary, do I actually like it.
"commit to a final word count, say: 348 words"
("A process for writing short, but thorough, content", 2022-04-05)

**19. Deliberate bottlenecks.**
Write longhand, or on a phone with one finger, to slow the hand below the speed of the inner editor. Borrowed from Obama's memoir.
"computer word processors give the first draft too much polish"
("Writing, slow", 2023-04-11)

**20. "Write the picture".**
Take one photograph, then animate it in prose: give it context, put the subjects in motion, record what they say. Optionally to an exact word count.
"give it context, by animating the subjects, by showing us what they're doing and saying"
("A picture is worth (exactly) 400 words", 2022-07-21)

**21. The analog-to-digital loop.**
Keep two desks. Rest at the analog one (cutting, gluing, models, LEGO, paint by numbers) when blocked, then return to the screen.
"When you start to lose steam," he said, "head back to your analog station and play."
(Austin Kleon, "The analog-to-digital loop", 2022-07-11)

**22. Creative rest by taking direction.**
When stuck, stop making creative decisions and follow instructions instead. Trey Parker builds LEGO for exactly this reason.
"start taking creative direction"
("A rejuvenating form of Creative Rest", 2022-02-22)

**23. The second swipe file.**
Alongside the file of classic ads, keep a mental file of observed people: the tired daughter, the impatient granddaughter, the grateful old woman.
"You need to observe people," said Betuel
("Your "other" swipe file", 2021-01-20)

**24. Sonder as a pre-writing warm-up.**
Browse street photography before writing to induce the recognition that strangers have inner lives as dense as yours, which is the emotional precondition for writing to strangers well.
"these are the people your copy speaks to: strangers, extras"
("Browse these photos before writing copy", 2023-03-21)

**25. The three-step curiosity headline.**
Make a valuable promise, tease the value, embellish the benefit. His worked example is Sugarman's BluBlocker ad, which sold $20 million of sunglasses.
"Make a valuable promise" / "Tease the value" / "Embellish the benefit"
("Proven headline formula", 2020-10-09)

**26. The impossible headline.**
Make a preposterous claim or promise a baffling outcome, then write the story first and reverse-engineer the headline so the loop closes elegantly.
"An impossible headline deliberately makes a preposterous claim"
("Impossible headlines", 2023-05-02)

**27. The 9 rules of fascinations.**
Write 7x more than needed (Parris Lampropoulos writes 140 to use 20); prove with specificity; prove with a source; clarity over concision; simple words; the prospect's own language; avoid transactional words like "buy"; avoid work words like "learn"; vary the frame with filters.
"Writing fascinations," said internet marketing pioneer, Ken McCarthy, "is the single-most important copywriting skill."
("How to write a compelling sentence", 2023-11-21)

**28. Periods instead of exclamation marks.**
Strip the exclamation marks; the claim itself should carry the voltage. Ben Settle's version: if the thing is true and needed, saying it plainly is already exciting.
"truth is the ultimate exclamation mark"
("The most powerful element in advertising", 2023-07-06)

**29. Invisible dialogue tags.**
Use only "said" or "asked", never said-bookisms or adverbs, and omit the tag when the speaker is clear.
"Your tags should be invisible."
("How to write better dialogue", 2021-06-25)

**30. One person, not a persona.**
Replace the composite avatar with a single real individual whose point of view becomes the source of truth for the page.
"A persona is a Frankenstein"
(Sean D'Souza, "Your source of truth (when creating ads)", 2023-06-22)

## Classic direct-response lineage

He treats the canon as a set of working tools, not history. Almost every name arrives with one operable lesson and one quotable line.

**Claude Hopkins.** Two lessons. First, preemptive claims: Hopkins sold Schlitz by advertising "Every Bottle Sterilized" and "Bottled Purity" at a time when every brewer sterilised, which created both brand association and doubt about competitors. Second, the permanence of psychology, which is the foundation of Shleyner's whole argument that old ads still work:
"Human nature is perpetual. In most respects it is the same today as in the time of Caesar. So the principles of psychology are fixed and enduring."
He also takes Hopkins' line on curiosity as "one of the strongest human incentives", and uses it to justify impossible headlines. Recommended reading: Scientific Advertising and My Life in Advertising.

**John Caples.** The lesson is that a headline's job is to signal value, not to shock:
"The best headlines," said Caples, "appeal to people's self-interest, or give news."
From this he builds two trigger-word lists. Self-interest: why, sale, save, easy, quick, results, proven, how to, bargain, guarantee, last chance. News: new, now, at last, it's here, discover, breaking, just arrived, introducing, announcing. He also uses Caples' most famous headline as an example of the dramatic register: "They Laughed When I Sat Down At the Piano, But When I Started to Play!" The surrounding argument matters more than the list: when a mentee objected that trigger words would make him sound unoriginal, Shleyner's answer came from Bob Bly,
"We are not in the business of being original... we are in the business of reusing things that work."
(Bob Bly, "The best headlines", 2021-12-21)

**David Ogilvy.** Four separate lessons.
Long copy: he quotes "In print advertising, long copy sells more than short copy" and then supplies the missing why, which is interest, perceived value and the sheer space required to answer objections. He is careful to keep Ogilvy's qualifier, "in print advertising".
The 10 tips memo: he devotes a six-part series to unpacking it, including "Write the way you talk. Naturally.", "Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs.", "Never use jargon words like reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally. They are hallmarks of a pretentious ass." and "If you want ACTION, don't write. Go and tell the guy what you want." His takeaway from the last one is that writing scales but one-to-one persuasion is beaten by eye contact.
Competing with the immortals: from a 1983 interview with John Crichton, Ogilvy's three parting instructions to young creatives:
"Be more ambitious." "Compete with the immortals." and "The more you know about the product, the more likely you are to sell it."
The unconscious: "Most great ideas come from the unconscious," and Ogilvy's admission that "I never wrote an ad in my life in the office."
("Compete with the immortals", 2023-05-18; "The most important issue of the year", 2023-12-18 series)

**Eugene Schwartz.** Three lessons, all about humility.
Connectivity: "A better word for 'creativity' is 'connectivity.'" This is the single most repeated quotation in the corpus and the spine of his creativity doctrine.
Channelling demand, delivered second-hand by Ken McCarthy at a tribute panel: "We're not paddling, we're sailing. We put up our sails and let the wind take us."
Hearing rather than inventing: "You don't have to have great ideas," Schwartz said, "if you can hear great ideas." Shleyner illustrates it with Schwartz interviewing a client for nearly four hours, then writing an ad that was about 70 percent the client's own words, with a headline that was a direct quote. The ad sold out the product.
The window metaphor is the fourth: words in advertising should be invisible, like clean glass, so the reader sees the product and not the smear. And the boundary line:
"if you want to write poetry, if you want to write prose, if you want to write novels, and if you want to write literature, go outside of advertising"
("Copywriters are sailing", 2022-05-05; "Infinite ideas in 3 steps", 2020-10-29; "Want more marketing ideas than you could ever use?", 2020-08-19)

**Robert Collier and Victor Schwab.** Honest note: in this era both appear only as recommended reading, not as taught lessons. Collier's *The Letter Book* and Schwab's *How to Write a Good Advertisement* are listed in the 35-book recommendation issue ("35 books I feel good recommending", 2021-11-08) and in the 18-book copywriting list, with no extract, no quotation and no technique attached. Anything more detailed attributed to them from this corpus would be invention. If you need their doctrine, source it elsewhere.

**Gary Bencivenga.** The most technically useful material in the corpus, and it arrives three times.
The guarantee headline for his new agency: "Announcing: an ad agency that guarantees to beat your best ad by at least 10 percent, or you pay us nothing." He then promised to refund the client's media spend as well. Shleyner uses this to introduce CRIT, and quotes Bencivenga's own adjectives for the process: "odious" and "sadistic" and "devastating" and "weird" and "cumbersome". The payoff line:
"But when it's finally finished," he said, "your client will have a campaign they could run for years"
The silver split test: Bencivenga argued with Dan Rosenthal over "may" versus "will" in a silver-price headline. Rosenthal's "may" won by roughly 200 percent. The lesson taught is counterintuitive and precise: a hedged claim outperformed a confident one because it was believable.
The fascination book: "The Doctor's Vest-Pocket Sampler of Natural Remedies", mailed to 30 million people, a free booklet that sold an expensive book. Lesson: "If you want to compel The Reader, make her curious." He later compiled 174 of Bencivenga's bullets into a paid product called Bencivenga Bullets.
On rarity, quoted about Doug D'Anna: "Writers who can consistently create successful direct response campaigns are as rare as trumpeter swans."

**Joe Sugarman.** Two lessons. The first sentence's only job is to earn the second sentence:
"The purpose of the 1st sentence in an ad is to get you to read the 2nd one."
And short headlines create curiosity by compression. He lists seven Sugarman headlines as evidence: "Magic Baloney", "Pet Plane", "Mail Order Mansion", "Hungarian Conspiracy", "Vision Breakthrough", "Gold Space Chains", "HOT", then quotes Sugarman: "My first sentences are so short, they almost aren't sentences." On failure: "My failures far outnumbered my successes," he said, "But it was through these failures that I received a very costly education." Also the origin of the sentence-to-sentence pull: each line exists only to buy the next line.

**Gary Halbert.** Present as a transcription target and a book (*The Boron Letters*, letters to his son from prison), plus one inheritance: the copywork prescription came bundled with Halbert, Bencivenga and Caples as the three hands to copy.

**Bob Bly.** Two contributions. The 50 percent Day-Timer win, where a specific promise with trigger words beat an abstract one, and the originality line above. He is also the source of Shleyner's default answer to "should headlines be outrageous": no, but they must "make or imply a fairly big promise".

**Drayton Bird.** Used as authority for the constancy of psychology rather than for technique:
"Human psychology is constant. The principles of copywriting transcend cultures, mediums, and generations."
Ogilvy's right-hand copywriter, author of *Commonsense Direct & Digital Marketing*, and the subject of a ten-essay micro-series ("Drayton Bird's 10 Copywriting Commandments") that is referenced but not reproduced in these files.

**Bill Bernbach.** Two lessons, both structural. In 1960 he put copywriter and art director in a room together instead of passing work under a door, producing the VW Think Small campaign; the lesson is that the idea comes first and comes from collaboration. And the line that anchors the anti-hype argument:
"The most powerful element in advertising," said copywriting great Bill Bernbach, "is truth."

**David Abbott.** The Chivas Regal "Father's Day" ad is treated as the high-water mark of show-don't-tell. Abbott's own account is the lesson:
"This ad is about Chivas Regal," said Abbott, "but it's also about me and my father."
Shleyner's reading is that the ad works because it "vividly echoes" the reader's own experience, and because it withholds detail, leaving holes for the reader's memories. He reads it twice, once as a son (pain, estrangement) and once as a father (aspiration), and uses that double reading as proof that one text can carry two opposite emotions.

**Others used as instruments rather than ancestors.** Bob Stone (the 7-step letter), Kim Krause Schwalm (40/40/20, "this benefit is a bit flat"), Ken McCarthy (fascinations, channeling demand, *The System Club Letters*), Parris Lampropoulos (7x volume on fascinations), Clayton Makepeace and John Carlton and Vin Montello (mentorship lineage), Doug D'Anna (specialisation: thirty years in financial copy meant "I have never had to reinvent the wheel on every project"), Ben Settle (conditions, brevity, "The worst thing your content can be is a fucking bore"), Dave Trott (*Predatory Thinking*), Drew Eric Whitman (the Life-Force 8), Robert Cialdini (reciprocity, pre-suasion), Al Ries and Jack Trout (positioning).

The Life-Force 8, quoted from Whitman, is the terminus of the dimensionalization drill: survival and life extension; food and drink; freedom from fear, pain and danger; physical companionship; comfortable living conditions; superiority and winning; care of loved ones; social approval.
("VeryGoodCopy is BACK!", 2023-01-26)

## Headline and hook patterns

**The impossible claim, closed by the story.** The model is a 1941 Hamilton watch ad:
"We waited 21 years for a train… and missed it!"
The construction method is explicit: write the story first, then reverse-engineer a headline that makes the story's resolution feel inevitable. Other examples he cites: "How to clean out a drawer without removing its contents", and his own "Don't write another headline before reading this".
("Impossible headlines", 2023-05-02)

**The hedged prediction.** "2 reasons why the price of SILVER may rise steeply". One word, "may", produced roughly a 200 percent lift over "will". The pattern generalises: number plus reason plus conditionally framed outcome.
("This may explode your copy's response", 2022-04-28)

**The question that implies a mechanism.** Bencivenga's "Has This Man Really Discovered the Secret of Inevitable Wealth?" The word "really" does the work; it implies the reader has already heard the claim and should be suspicious of it.

**Direct address to a self-identified group.** "To People Who Want to Write But Can't Get Started" and "To The Man Who Is Afraid To Let His Dreams Come True". The pattern is "To [person] who [wants X but is blocked by Y]".

**The news frame.** "What Everybody Ought to Know About the Stock Market", "Will There Be BOOM and More INFLATION Ahead?", "Vision Breakthrough". Caples' second category, and the reason "announcing", "introducing", "at last" and "new" sit in his trigger list.

**Sensory or metaphorical compression.** "Tastes Like You Just Picked It!" (United Fruit) and "Banishes Acne!" (Pears Soap). The second is interesting because it is a metaphor doing the work of a claim.

**The first-person demonstration.** Sugarman's "When I put on the pair of glasses, what I saw I could not believe. Nor will you." The structure is: I did the thing, the result is unbelievable, and you will feel the same. This is the ancestor of most of Shleyner's own subject lines.

**Reframe a commodity as an experience.** "A skin you love to touch" (Helen Lansdowne Resor, 1911), paired with the urgency of "Send now…", "Write today...", "Begin the following treatment tonight...". His point is that the language of direct response has not changed in over a hundred years.

**His own subject-line habits.** Four recurring shapes, all visible in the export:
- The withheld object: "Most Valuable Player:", "This is it:", "Testing:", "Something special :)".
- The number plus artefact: "9 writing "rules" (and 57 examples):", "8 compelling ways to write a headline:", "16 copywriting things", "12 revelations on my way to 300 VGC articles:".
- The value judgement on someone else's copy: "This 115-word sales letter is a poetic masterpiece:", "Horrible, horrible copy:", "One of the great ads of all time, explained:".
- The second-person promise of a capability: "This will make your Readers happy:", "Do this to develop your copywriting voice:", "How to write immersive stories:".

The colons are near-universal. The register is spoken, not literary. And the curiosity is almost always about a thing he has and you do not yet: a letter, a rule, an exercise, a discovery.

## Emotional and narrative technique

**Realism: actions and dialogue only.** His house style. No interior monologue, no explanation of feeling, no adjectives doing the work of evidence. The model passage is his son's birth, where the narrator never states fear:
"I just saw my wife. And heard the monitor. And felt my throat closing."
Then the reversal arrives as a fact, not a reflection: "heart rate stabilized". He explains the mechanism explicitly: because the reader cannot see inside a character's head in life either, withholding interiority makes the scene feel real, and the gaps become the reader's own memories.
("Every marketer should study this writing style", 2021-08-16)

**Facts as emotional carriers.** In the blood-draw vignette the emotional information is entirely physical:
"My mouth is dry." / "My palms are wet." / "My mouth isn't dry anymore."
He labels these three categories in the text itself: facts, actions, dialogue. Nothing else is permitted.
("How to write immersive stories", 2022-09-14)

**The zoom-in as the answer to a huge abstraction.** Asked what love is like, he does not define it. He describes rolling onto his wife's side of the bed and hugging her pillow because it smells like her shampoo. The doctrine follows the story:
"Next time you need to efficiently articulate a huge concept, whether it's about love or business or anything else, zoom in."
("Love, explained by a copywriter", 2023-02-14)

**The zero-ending.** Carver's "Cathedral" ends on "It's really something," with the narrator's eyes still closed. Shleyner's analysis is that the reader does not witness the epiphany, she has it. He reproduces the technique in his own endings, which are typically one line, often one word: "Go on." / "Amen." / "Your move, Chief." / "Yeh, ok."

**Primal stakes.** From *Save the Cat*: husbands and wives, fathers and daughters, mothers and sons. The illustration is a Waco reporter describing a wounded ATF agent asking for his wedding ring to be pushed back onto his finger, and the reporter's only thought being his own wife. The rule:
"Show your prospect a spouse, a child, a father or mother, and she will see her people, her life."
("Primal copywriting", 2023-04-18)

**Conflict and change, marked in the margin.** Snyder's "><" and "+/-" notation, applied as a diagnostic to any scene: one conflict only, and an emotional reversal from plus to minus or minus to plus. He demonstrates it on a Super Bowl party anecdote about a loud friend, then names the symbols.
("All compelling stories have these things in common", 2023-02-28)

**Dialogue as the whole scene.** In "Writing rules" the entire argument about literary convention is carried by a beach conversation with a friend, with no quotation marks at all, mimicking Sally Rooney. The doctrine is stated by the narrator in the last line only:
"Yeh well, "writing rules" is an oxymoron, I say."
("Writing rules:", 2021-10-05)

**Dialogue with subtext and interruption.** The mentorship call with Kim opens on "you look just like your picture, same hat". The dentist scene opens with "Do you floss?" and ends with termites eating teeth. He consistently enters a scene mid-conversation rather than setting it up.

**The reveal-by-withholding ending.** "Horrible, horrible copy" runs two tender scenes (grandmother pouring imaginary tea into his cupped hand; grandmother dying) and then cuts to a pole advert reading "CANCER SOLUTIONS DISCOVERED / 97% SUCCESS RATE / 100% NATURAL". The emotional payload is three words:
"oh so you quietly cured cancer? fuck u."
("Horrible, horrible copy", 2022-02-08)

**The turn.** Every essay has one, and it is usually marked typographically rather than transitionally. He breaks the story, states the principle in a single line, then returns to the story to close. Examples: "This is called a "zero-ending."" / "It's called: "Realism"" / "Here's the trick, a technique."

**Ordinary drama over spectacle.** The Fred Rogers Senate testimony is used to license smallness: a haircut, sibling jealousy, family anger, are more dramatic than gunfire. His application to copy is that the copywriter's material is the same small ordinary drama, handled calmly rather than sensationally.
("The inner drama of childhood", 2022-05-25)

**Self-deprecation as credibility.** He routinely appears as the fool: zoning out in a school nurse's office, being rejected by HubSpot and Facebook, picking skin off his thumb, hiding behind a vacuum cleaner, cringing at his own recorded talk. The pattern is consistent enough to be a technique: the narrator is never the hero of his own anecdotes.

**Profanity as register control.** Deployed sparingly and always at an emotional peak, never decoratively: "fuck u" at the cancer ad, "fugged it up" at the HubSpot rejection, "It's so fucking hard to get comfortable" in the Seinfeld quotation.

**Repetition and anaphora as pacing.** The pipe-organ letter is the model, and he analyses it in the text: four "It gives" clauses, three items per clause, then the turn to "It finally gives itself." His stated lesson is that the rhythm is what makes an inanimate object feel like a person.
("This 115-word sales letter is a poetic masterpiece", 2022-09-20)

## Anything superseded

This era revises itself repeatedly. Where the later material in Part 1 speaks, it wins.

**1. The weekly cadence promise.** June 2021: "I'm gonna send a newsletter every week," I said. November 2022: "I won't be sending these anymore", announcing the end of the launch-email sequence. January 2023: "VeryGoodCopy is BACK!". The commitment softened from a schedule to a rhythm, with explicit breaks (a December 2023 note says the regular micro-essay newsletter returns in January 2024). Replaced by: publish when there is something worth sending, and say so when you stop.

**2. Long copy as an unqualified law.** October 2020 states it flatly as a heading, "Long copy sells more than short copy", then quietly keeps Ogilvy's qualifier in the body ("In print advertising"). By 2022-2023 two corrections are in place: length is justified by being "transportive" and by letting the reader fantasise, not by a universal rule; and Rule #4 of his own fascination method is "Clarity beats concision". Replaced by: length is a function of format and of how much must be answered, and clarity is the constraint that governs it.

**3. Adjective and adverb absolutism.** In 2021 he passes on Stephen King's "Adverbs are not my friends" and quotes Zinsser approvingly on adjectives being "unnecessary". In March 2023 he explicitly walks it back: "I tend to agree with this. But I'm not draconian about it. Adjectives are not a writing sin. Only misplaced adjectives are a sin." The Justin Schmidt wasp-sting description ("Burning, throbbing, and lonely.") is the proof text. Replaced by: the well-placed adjective is a precision instrument.

**4. "Writing rules" are an oxymoron.** October 2021 ends on that line, using Sally Rooney's unpunctuated dialogue as proof that conventions are breakable. November 2023 publishes "9 writing "rules" (and 57 examples)" and October 2023 publishes "The rules of "real" writing", both with the scare quotes retained but the rules fully codified. Replaced by: rules exist, they are conventions rather than laws, and you should know them before breaking them.

**5. Persona-driven targeting.** The 2022-2023 material teaches segmentation by proven desire, and the 2020 material talks comfortably about audiences and markets. By June 2023 he rejects the composite outright: "A persona is a Frankenstein", and "So I don't lean on personas to create ads". Replaced by: one real individual as source of truth, because a composite is nobody.

**6. His own affiliate purity.** May 2023: "I started VeryGoodCopy nearly 10 years ago and I've never used my platform as an affiliate. It never felt right." In the same email he launches an affiliate bundle with Kevin Rogers, describing it as VeryGoodCopy's first affiliate offer after nearly 10 years. Replaced by: affiliate offers are acceptable when the product is one he would recommend anyway, and the earlier abstinence is framed as caution rather than principle.

**7. Quantity as a bare virtue.** 2020-2021 states it without qualification: fifty vignettes earn an A, and "The people with the highest quantity of submissions always, always, produce the highest quality work." By 2023 it is a two-stage process: "Quality is the product of quantity", followed immediately by Parris Lampropoulos writing seven times what the brief needs and then paring down. Replaced by: generate at volume, then select ruthlessly.

**8. AI as a non-threat, stated absolutely.** February 2023: "AI may eventually replace some writing. But it cannot replace writers" and "So will AI replace writers? No, it will not." June 2023 softens to "I'll never tell you not to use AI writing tools. Because I use them", with the newsletter sponsored by an AI company, and the metaphor reduced from "cannot" to "AI is a genius, cocky, scared kid". Note also that the May 2023 claims "AI makes copywriters superhuman" and "AI will not replace direct response copywriters" are sponsor copy by Kevin Rogers in a takeover, not Shleyner's own voice, and should not be cited as his position. Replaced by: AI is a daily tool and a real accelerant, and the defensible human asset is specific lived experience.

**9. Engagement mechanics as a permanent fixture.** Through 2020-2022 nearly every issue closes with a request to like and comment on LinkedIn, often with a giveaway, a repost raffle, or a prize for the best comment. By late 2023 the ask is shortened and the raffles narrow to reposts. Replaced by: a lighter, less transactional close, with the reciprocity argument ("Marketing & giving", 2022-01-03) retained as the justification rather than the prize.

**10. The free-newsletter-only model.** 2020-2021 routes the most specific material into the VGC Plus Patreon ("my most specific, in-depth, and actionable content will only be inside my newsletter", 2021-10-27). 2022-2023 routes depth into a single video course with 86 lessons, and gives the newsletter away free. Replaced by: one flagship paid product, with the newsletter as the top of the funnel.

## Coverage note

**What is well covered.** The direct-response canon as Shleyner uses it (Hopkins, Caples, Ogilvy, Schwartz, Bencivenga, Sugarman, Bly, Bernbach, Abbott, Bird); his own craft doctrine on clarity, images, dimensionalization, fascinations, realism, dialogue and endings; the 40/40/20 and Bob Stone frameworks in full; the creativity-as-connection argument; and his publishing and audience-growth mechanics, including the LinkedIn full-post repurposing discovery.

**What is thin or absent.** No systematic treatment of research methodology beyond three research questions in "Want more marketing ideas than you could ever use?"; no offer-architecture or pricing doctrine; almost nothing on email deliverability, list hygiene or segmentation mechanics beyond the list/offer/creative split; no testing methodology beyond "test early and often" and one split-test anecdote; no B2B or SaaS-specific landing page structure (that lives in the paid course, referenced but not reproduced); no client-work case studies with numbers, apart from his own and Justin Welsh's conversion figures.

**Named but not taught.** Robert Collier, Victor Schwab, Drayton Bird's ten commandments, Claude Hopkins' full Scientific Advertising argument, and the contents of the paid courses. Treat these as citations, not summaries.

**Structural caveats.** About 90 of the 285 emails are promotional, and roughly 20 are duplicate sends. Subject lines were A/B tested by resending the same issue minutes apart, so near-identical emails with different subjects are one issue, not two. Some internal headings do not match the subject line (the January 2023 "VeryGoodCopy is BACK!" issue is internally titled "Tapping into your prospect's very humanity"). Dates are header send dates in local time and occasionally appear out of order within a file. Two emails dated 2023 (22 Nov, 21 Nov) are the same issue under different subjects, as are the two November 2023 "great ads" emails and the two June 2023 "7-step formula" emails.

**Voice and safety note.** Several emails are written by guests or sponsors (Kevin Rogers in the May 2023 takeover, Ann Handley in interview, Drayton Bird in the micro-series). Attribute carefully. The corpus also contains a first-person account of plagiarism and a public accusation against an unnamed writer; treat that episode as reported allegation rather than established fact.
