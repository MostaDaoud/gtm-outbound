# Harry Dry - Email Corpus Reference

Harry Dry writes Marketing Examples, a newsletter and site about advertising and copy, and he is the most quoted practitioner of the show-me-the-ad, then-tell-me-why-it-works school of copywriting. This corpus holds eight emails sent from harry@marketingexamples.com, dated 20 February 2024 to 1 May 2025: five numbered 3-2-1 issues, one essay ("I'm back"), one re-permission email, and one countdown issue ("One hundred days"). The format is fixed and severe: an intro in his own voice, a three-bullet sponsor slot, three copywriting tips, two short examples, one favourite tweet, a sign-off, a song of the week. What makes the writing distinctive is that the analysis and the demonstration are the same act: he states a rule in eight words, then hands you an ad that obeys it, and the ad is usually funnier and stranger than the rule. He writes short sentences on purpose, uses white space as punctuation, and treats a newsletter like a magician's table, showing you the trick while performing it.

## How to cite

Cite as (Subject, Mon DD YYYY), using the subject line and the send date exactly as they appear in the file header, with the date normalized to three-letter month. Quotations are verbatim, including his typos, his asterisks for emphasis, and his masked profanity. Where he is quoting somebody else (a film, a tweet, a founder, an ad), the citation still points at the email, and the quote is introduced as his quotation, not as his own sentence. Example: ("The result is lots of bad ideas. But I'd rather bad ideas than boring ones.", One hundred days, May 01 2025).

## Doctrine: the rules he states

**Write it on purpose.** "Just try to make it sound like you wrote it that way on purpose." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). He takes the line from Arthur Howitzer Jr in The French Dispatch and makes it the founding rule of the whole method. The opposite of on-purpose is not bad writing, it is writing that happened: "Most sentences are not written 'on purpose'. They happen." (same). His test is whether a sentence could only have come from this product, this writer, this moment.

**If it sounds like writing, rewrite it.** "It sounds like writing, so let's rewrite it." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). The trigger is a specific register, the press-release register, which he demonstrates on a stock phrase and then breaks. This is a portable editing instruction: when a line sounds like copy, the fix is not better adjectives but a different sentence.

**Start from what everyone else is saying, then break one piece of it.** "I start by writing down what everyone in said industry is doing or saying. Then I ask what piece can I break so it doesn't sound like anything else." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). Note the order: inventory first, rupture second. Provocation without an inventory is random; an inventory without rupture is a moodboard.

**Prefer bad ideas to boring ones.** "The result is lots of bad ideas. But I'd rather bad ideas than boring ones." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). This is a production rule, not a taste rule. It licenses volume in the first pass and puts the judgement in the selection, not the generation.

**Provocation has to come from the product.** "You are looking for provocativeness that stems from the product." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). He backs it with Bernbach: "You ARE right if you have him on his head to show how your product keeps things from falling out of his pockets." (same). The shock must be evidence of a feature. A stunt with no product inside it is the thing Bernbach was warning about.

**Do not start an idea with an adjective.** "Adjectives are okay. Just *ideas* don't start with them." (3-2-1: A (short) summer special, Jul 24 2024). His opening question for any ad is "How can I do the ad without any adjectives?" (same). Adjectives are permitted as seasoning; they are not permitted as the seed, because an adjective describes a category and a category is where distinctiveness goes to die.

**Features become benefits through "so what", then through contrast.** His three-step pattern is "Write down the feature", then "Ask, 'so what?' to find the benefit", then "Contrast the old way with your new way" (3-2-1: A (short) summer special, Jul 24 2024). The third step is the one most copywriters skip, and it is the one that produces the sentence: the benefit only becomes persuasive when the reader can see the previous world next to the new one.

**Never write a label. Write a tangible.** "Don't write 'labels'" followed by "Give me something tangible." (3-2-1: A (short) summer special, Jul 24 2024). A label is a word that classifies the thing (premium, artisanal, AI-powered); a tangible is a thing you could drop on your foot. He credits fellow Harry from DTC Pages for the rule, which suggests it is a shared craft norm rather than a personal quirk.

**Most copy needs more conflict.** "Nearly all copy you write will benefit from more conflict" (3-2-1: Marketing Examples is BACK!, Feb 20 2024). Stated flatly, with no elaboration in the text, and then demonstrated for the rest of the issue: the agency ad that beats "qUaLitY LeAdS", AG1 against the temptation of mango flavour, a founder against an avatar. Conflict is his default additive.

**A title is a promise, and the test is betrayal.** "A good title makes a promise." Then the diagnostic question: "Would you feel cheated if the content breaks the promise?" (3-2-1: Marketing Examples is BACK!, Feb 20 2024). "That's when you have a good title." (same). This is a usefulness test disguised as an honesty test. It also explains his subject lines: they promise a specific pleasure (an argument, a number, a weird noun) and the email pays it.

**Big ideas are conviction, not creativity.** "Big ideas are less about creativity and more about conviction." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). He goes further: "In fact, I think your conviction in an idea is more important than the idea itself." (same). The operational consequence is in the next line, which is about behaviour in a meeting rather than talent.

**Be willing to be the person who says it.** "You have to be willing to stop the meeting, stand on the table, with just six words on an A4 pad, and say 'this is the idea'." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). And when the room objects that you cannot get someone to lie under a suspended Volvo: "I guess, it's me then" (same). Conviction is expressed as ownership of execution, not as volume in the pitch.

**Micromanage the execution.** "And then you micromanage every word, every line, every take, every edit." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). This is the clause that separates his position from the ideas-person cliche. The idea is not finished at the idea; it is finished at the last edit.

**There is no prompt for conviction.** "There is no AI prompt for conviction." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). His entire objection to generated work is compressed into that sentence: the missing input is not information or fluency, it is a person who cares enough to insist.

**Hunt sentences that say nothing.** "The biggest mistake I see is writing sentences that say nothing" (3-2-1: Deliberately short, The Dream 100, Olives, Feb 27 2024). The remedy is perceptual, not compositional: "To stop writing them, you've got to spot them." (same). He turns the drill into a game, seven sentences about cars, five empty, two with content, answer at the end.

**When stuck, build a comparison table.** "Feel stuck? Draw up a comparison table" (3-2-1: Deliberately short, The Dream 100, Olives, Feb 27 2024). The payoff is diagnostic: "You'll realise what you're up against and what makes you unique." (same). The table forces you to name the alternatives, which is where differentiation actually comes from.

**Judge design in the wild, not in the tool.** "Get off Photoshop. It never looks like the pdf." (3-2-1: Marketing Examples is BACK!, Feb 20 2024). Then: "Stick it up against others. Which one do you read?" (same). Context beats craft review. A layout that wins in isolation can lose in a feed.

**Give them one way to buy.** "There's ONE way to buy Athletic Greens." (3-2-1: Marketing Examples is BACK!, Feb 20 2024). "One button to press. Idiot proof." (same). He pre-empts the objection that choice is a kindness ("But options are harmless, right? You'd rather a choice.") and answers it with Trout and Ries: "The power of your brand is inversely proportional to its scope." (same).

**Narrow the scope to widen the brand.** That Trout and Ries law is the theoretical spine of the AG1 example, and its logic of concentration runs through the Dream 100 story too. The practical version: "Imagine AG1 mango. Or strawberry kiwi. Different sizes. Stick packs. 'Let's make a bar'. Now it's just another nutrition company." (same). Every added variant subtracts meaning.

**Impact, then communication, then persuasion.** Dave Trott's triangle, as he relays it: "every ad needs three things", "You've got to get noticed", "You've got to tell me what", "You've got to tell me why" (3-2-1: Make this ad better, Rewriting CTAs, Dave Trott, Mar 13 2024). His diagnosis of the market: "Most ads just do the 'communication' part." (same). This is his most reusable checklist and the one he applies to real ads in the same issue.

**Entertain to educate.** "You have to entertain to educate because the other way around doesn't work" (Mar 13 2024), attributed to Walt Disney. He uses it to justify Innocent's product launch ("Did we mention it's blue") over the standard launch format. The order matters: entertainment is not a garnish added after the lesson, it is the delivery mechanism for the lesson.

**Attention is part of the job.** He answers the objection before it is made, insisting "it's not the job of a display ad to 'get attention'" and asking "when was the last time you clicked a display ad." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). This is a doctrine about professional honesty, not about taste: if nobody looks, nothing else you did exists.

**One second slowed down beats two hours summed up.** "One second slowed down is more interesting than two hours summed up." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). This is his rule for posters and any compressed format. Specificity at a single instant outperforms summary, because a summary has no camera in it.

**Do not ask for the meeting.** The strongest cold-email rule in the corpus arrives as a story: he got 97 cold emails in a month and replied to one, the one that did not ask for anything. Lizzy's version: "I stopped asking for a quick call and clarified I wasn't asking for anything. Responses went from 5% to 25%." (3-2-1: A (short) summer special, Jul 24 2024). His gloss inverts the proverb: "Don't ask, don't get!" (same).

**Compete on the number of people doing it your way.** "How many people are trying to reach the prospect exactly the same way as me?" (Jul 24 2024). "The lower the number the better chance you've got." (same). This reframes differentiation as a counting exercise. "Automating sludge? Hundreds!" (same), whereas finding a company whose problem you solve, solving it without permission, and posting about it is "Probably just you." (same).

**Let the proof be the pudding.** "The proof is the pudding." (Jul 24 2024). The phrase is a deliberate mangling of the proverb about the proof of the pudding being in the eating, and it carries the argument: send the work, not the promise of work. The whole Search Intelligence example later is the same rule at agency scale.

**Make the number bigger by changing the timeframe.** "Want a number to feel bigger? Use a bigger timeframe." (Would you like to keep getting my newsletter?, Apr 19 2024). A one-line arithmetic trick for metrics that are technically true. He gives no example, which makes it feel like a note to self.

**Choose a deadline over an idea.** "I don't need more ideas, I need a deadline." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). Released as a confession attached to a public ship date (7 August, one hundred days out) and a plan for a "surgical summer" (same). The newsletter itself is the accountability device.

**Comfort is the trap.** "I feel safe. No blank page, no writer's block. The tools comfort me." Then: "No, they are the life preserver that sinks you." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). Unlimited words, templates, drones, testing tools: all of it removes the discomfort that used to signal that something real was at stake. "You are supposed to feel afraid. I've never gotten very far without PANICKING!" (same).

**Do not lecture people who do not read.** On list hygiene: "I only want to email folks who read the emails." (Apr 19 2024). "So I'm deleting inactive subscribers." (same). The re-permission email is itself a demonstration of the doctrine: one ask, one click, no guilt, and a free sample of the product attached.

**Keep it short and never sell in it.** "I work hard to keep it short, it's never promotional, and I'd love you to stay!" (Apr 19 2024). The sentence is doing double duty: it is the value proposition of the newsletter and the reason to opt back in. Note that the issue containing it has a sponsor, so "never promotional" means never promoting himself.

**Target the few who matter.** "Write down your dream 100 clients. Do anything you can to win them." (Feb 27 2024). The evidence is in the arithmetic of the story: 2,200 prospects, of which "167 companies are responsible for 95% of all ad sales." (same). Narrowing is not a moral position, it is a response to a distribution curve.

**Nothing is off the table when the prize is big.** "When you're negotiating with millionares nothing is off the table." (Feb 27 2024, spelling as printed). He then tells the Walt Batansky story to show what "nothing" means: buying 100 shares at $6 to get into a shareholder meeting. The rule is about permission, not about ethics.

## Techniques with worked examples

**The before/after rewrite, performed live.** Mechanism: show the sentence everyone writes, then replace it with one that could only belong to that brand, without explaining the difference. "We're excited to announce the future of photography" becomes "The industry formerly known as 'photography' has ceased to exist." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). The lesson is carried entirely by the juxtaposition; a paragraph of theory would have weakened it.

**The triple substitution.** Mechanism: line up three category cliches and three provocations, so the reader infers the rule from the pattern. "'Refresh your thirst' → 'Murder your thirst'" (Liquid Death), "'Buy this jacket' → 'Don't buy this jacket'" (Patagonia), "'Party game for friends' → '... horrible people'" (Cards Against Humanity), all in (One hundred days, May 01 2025). Three examples is his standard dose: enough to establish a pattern, few enough to stay readable.

**The one-second reading.** Mechanism: describe a poster by the single instant it contains rather than by its message. "One second slowed down is more interesting than two hours summed up." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). He offers no further analysis of the poster, which is the point: the reader is invited to look, and the looking is the argument.

**The attention-intrigue-action sequence.** Mechanism: narrate your own passage through an ad as a three-beat story, so the structure is felt rather than asserted. "Three words appear. 'Hey marketing people.' You have my attention." then "'Branded pens don't taste this good'. Now, I'm intrigued." then "Next comes action, 'Customise a Tony's bar'." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). Closing proof of effect: "I didn't know what custom chocolate was two minutes ago. Now I'm on Tony's site, ordering ten bars." (same).

**The open loop with an answer at the end.** Mechanism: pose a puzzle, withhold the solution, and place the solution after the sponsor and the examples so the reader has to travel through the issue to get it. "Here's a rubber. Make this ad better." then "Answer at end of email." (Mar 13 2024). Same device as "Below are seven sentences about cars. Five say nothing. Two say something." (Feb 27 2024), answered with "Answer: 3 and 6 say something" (same).

**The quiz that teaches the criterion.** Mechanism: make the reader perform the discrimination you want them to learn, then name the criterion afterwards. "One of these videos has eighteen million views" then "Take a guess." then "Answer: How to Start a Speech" then "A good title makes a promise." (Feb 20 2024). The rule lands because the reader has already committed to a guess.

**The feature-benefit-contrast ladder.** Mechanism: three lines, each a step, no prose: "Write down the feature", then "Ask, 'so what?' to find the benefit", then "Contrast the old way with your new way" (Jul 24 2024). This is the most directly copyable template in the corpus: it can be run on any product in about ninety seconds and it always produces a sentence with a before and an after in it.

**Objection removal inside the button.** Mechanism: put the reader's hesitation into the CTA itself, so the click answers the fear. As relayed by Growth Insights: "Get started" becomes "Get started for just $X"; "Start free!" becomes "Start free! No CC required." (Growth Insights #030, May 20 2025, attributed there to Harry Dry). The technique is one clause long and needs no design work, which is why it spreads.

**The mock-quote of the enemy.** Mechanism: write the competitor's sentence in your own copy and then answer it in the voice of a annoyed customer. "'We're a brand agency'" then "'No s***. I just googled that'" (Mar 13 2024). The mask on the profanity is part of the voice: it reads as a person restraining themselves, not as a brand.

**Sound as argument.** Mechanism: use a nonsense phrase as the headline and let the rhythm carry the persuasion. "Bou Bou Bou. Wow. Wow. Wow." (Mar 13 2024 and Mar 05 2024), reported as "their best performing ad" (same). He does not decode it, he just puts Trott's triangle next to it and lets the reader hear impact, communication and persuasion in three beats.

**The single-choice product page.** Mechanism: remove every option except one bundle, then say so plainly. "There's ONE way to buy Athletic Greens." (Feb 20 2024). Supporting detail does the persuasion: "Free shaker, free scooper, free delivery. Everything you need. One button to press. Idiot proof." (same). The four short sentences mimic the page.

**The status reversal.** Mechanism: contrast the buyer's current supplier (an avatar, a promise) with the seller's evidence (a person, a walkthrough). "An avatar in your inbox promising 'qUaLitY LeAdS'" versus "a founder giving a step-by-step breakdown of a 100 link client campaign in one take" (Feb 20 2024). The mixed-case mockery does the sneering so the sentence does not have to.

**The steak-on-a-plate metaphor.** Mechanism: import a concrete image from outside the category and let it organise the whole argument. "Straight up steak on a plate." (Feb 20 2024), borrowed from Casey Neistat's line about steakhouses and sauce: "Shitty franchises cover theirs in sauce and other shit to distract you from the fact you're eating dog food." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). The image recurs across two emails: the earlier issue uses it bare, and in the essay he quotes Casey Neistat spelling it out.

**The specific number as proof of life.** Mechanism: one precise, slightly awkward figure does more than any adjective. "167 companies are responsible for 95% of all ad sales." (Feb 27 2024). "A few months later Xerox order 104 full-color spreads." (same). "Four months in and impressions just hit 1.8 million / month." (Mar 13 2024). "Fery tells me revenue is $2.7M up on last year. Probably coincidental." (Feb 20 2024). The last one adds irony, which is how he avoids sounding like a case study.

**The parenthetical aside as personality.** Mechanism: break the sentence with a bracket that sounds like a person thinking, not a copywriter qualifying. "The trouble with brands on social media is (no matter how hard you try) you're a brand on social media." (Mar 13 2024). "Making this one took a 12 hour flight to Texas (and some)!" (One hundred days, May 01 2025). The bracket is where the voice lives.

**The flat declarative closer.** Mechanism: end a section on a three-to-six word sentence so the reader stops. "Probably just you." (Jul 24 2024). "The proof is the pudding." (same). "My train is coming." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). "Start there." (Jul 24 2024). These lines carry disproportionate weight because everything before them was longer.

**The weather opening.** Mechanism: open with a physical scene that has nothing to do with marketing, then pivot on a "but". "Live from London. Weather update. Trees shuddering, majestic hail." then "It's watch from the window weather." then "But I have a newsletter to write..." (Apr 19 2024). The scene buys intimacy and gives the issue a timestamp it could not otherwise have.

**The deadline confession.** Mechanism: announce a ship date and a private weakness in the same paragraph, so the reader becomes a witness. "I don't need more ideas, I need a deadline." (May 01 2025). Then the specific date, then the joke that deflates the grandeur: "It's going to be a surgical summer." (same). Accountability plus self-mockery is his standard opening move for big announcements.

**The recycled body.** Mechanism: an issue can be re-sent with a new subject and a new frame when the material is strong enough. The 5 March 2024 and 13 March 2024 issues share an almost identical body (Trott's triangle, Innocent, Slice, the same closing lines) under two different subjects. The re-permission email of 19 April 2024 also embeds the previous Monday's issue as a "refresher" (Apr 19 2024). He treats an email as a container for an argument, not as a disposable artifact.

## Headlines, hooks and subject lines

**The three-noun promise.** "3-2-1: Deliberately short, The Dream 100, Olives" (Feb 27 2024). Two useful nouns and one that does not belong. "Olives" is the hook: it is small, concrete and unexplained, so the reader opens the email to find out what olives have to do with B2B sales. The pattern is repeatable: two knowns and one stranger.

**The quoted fragment.** The subject runs 3-2-1: “Bou Bou Bou”, Pizza influencer, Rewrite this (Mar 05 2024). The subject begins mid-sound. A quotation mark signals that the email contains a found object rather than an opinion, which is a different promise from one that offers advice.

**The verb-first instruction.** "3-2-1: Make this ad better, Rewriting CTAs, Dave Trott" (Mar 13 2024). Two of the three items are verbs addressed to the reader ("Make", "Rewriting"), one is a name. The mix implies participation plus authority: you will do something, and a famous person will help.

**The revival.** "3-2-1: Marketing Examples is BACK!" (Feb 20 2024). All caps on the last word, exclamation mark, no explanation. It works because the archive is the product: the promise is simply more of the thing people already miss. In the body he does not oversell the return, he says: "I always get a little nervous writing the intro. Especially after a while." (same).

**The self-deprecating frame.** "3-2-1: A (short) summer special" (Jul 24 2024). The brackets lower the stakes and raise curiosity at once. He then over-delivers against the low promise, which is the oldest trick in the book and still works.

**The direct question.** "Would you like to keep getting my newsletter?" (Apr 19 2024). The subject is the entire ask. No cleverness, no urgency, no guilt. For a re-permission email this is close to optimal because any cleverness would read as manipulation at exactly the moment trust is being tested.

**The confession.** "I'm back" (Apr 01 2025). Two words, no explanation of the gap, and the body immediately acknowledges it: "To long-time readers thank you for your patience. To first-time readers a warm welcome." (same). The subject promises a person, not a topic.

**The deadline.** "One hundred days" (May 01 2025). A number and a unit, no verb. It reads like a countdown because it is one, and it makes the reader a witness to a public commitment without asking them for anything.

**Opening lines as hooks.** The first line after the greeting never summarises; it starts in motion. "I don't need more ideas, I need a deadline." (May 01 2025). "I'm bad at finishing stuff. Not quite right. Sleep on it." (Feb 27 2024). "Live from London. Weather update." (Apr 19 2024). "I always get a little nervous writing the intro." (Feb 20 2024). "Monday speeded by. I shall not delay further." (Mar 05 2024). Each is a fragment of a person's day, and each ends in a turn toward the content.

**Before/after pairs he shows.**

- "We're excited to announce the future of photography" / "The industry formerly known as 'photography' has ceased to exist." (One hundred days, May 01 2025)
- "Refresh your thirst" / "Murder your thirst" (same)
- "Buy this jacket" / "Don't buy this jacket" (same)
- "Party game for friends" / "... horrible people" (same)
- "Get started" / "Get started for just $X" (Growth Insights #030, May 20 2025, attributed to him there)
- "Start free!" / "Start free! No CC required." (same)
- "We're a brand agency" / Bou's "Bou Bou Bou. Wow. Wow. Wow." (Mar 13 2024), presented as the difference between communication alone and all three of Trott's legs
- most new product launches / Innocent's "Did we mention it's blue" (Mar 13 2024)
- most agency marketing / "Do great work", "Share how you did it", "Run as ad on founder's personal account" (Feb 20 2024)
- the standard launch with many options / "One button to press. Idiot proof." (Feb 20 2024)

**The read-time promise.** "Estimated read time: 2 minute 17 seconds." (May 01 2025). Also "2 minute 13 seconds" (Jul 24 2024), "3 minutes 3 seconds" (Feb 27 2024), "2 minutes 35 seconds" (Feb 20 2024), "1 minute 44 seconds" (Apr 19 2024). The singular "minute" where grammar wants the plural is not a typo he fixes. The device does three jobs: it promises brevity, it signals precision, and it makes the length itself a design decision.

## Teardowns and critiques

**Tony's display ad: diagnosed as a sequence, not a design.** His diagnosis is that the ad earns attention in three moves and each move is shorter than the last. The critique is written as his own browsing experience: "Three words appear. 'Hey marketing people.' You have my attention." then the joke, then the verb. The specific fix he is implicitly recommending is the first line: address the audience by name, in three words, before saying anything about the product. He also pre-empts the standard objection to display advertising, and the rebuttal is a question rather than a principle: "when was the last time you clicked a display ad." (One hundred days, May 01 2025).

**The Bou ad: diagnosed with Trott's triangle.** The critique is structural. Most ads fail because they stop at communication: "'We're a brand agency'" and the customer's reply "'No s***. I just googled that'" (Mar 13 2024). Bou's ad opens with impact ("Bou Bou Bou. Wow. Wow. Wow."), then communicates, then persuades, and the proof offered is commercial: Jesse from Bou "tells me it's their best performing ad" (same). The fix he recommends is not a rewrite, it is a checklist: get noticed, say what it is, say why it matters, in that order.

**Innocent's launch: diagnosed as entertainment debt.** "Most new product launches look something like this." Then: "Innocent's look like this." (Mar 13 2024). The diagnosis is that a launch usually asks the reader to care about a feature, and Innocent instead gives them a joke about a feature ("Did we mention it's blue"). His supporting principle is Disney's: "You have to entertain to educate because the other way around doesn't work" (same). The fix: find the one absurd thing about the product and lead with it.

**Slice's pizza influencer: diagnosed as a category problem, not a content problem.** "The trouble with brands on social media is (no matter how hard you try) you're a brand on social media." (Mar 13 2024). The fix he reports is a transfer of authorship, not a change of format: hire a person, "Give them the keys", and let them make the thing. "Out with posts of garlic bread. In with 'Take Me to Your Slice'." (same). He includes the result, "impressions just hit 1.8 million / month", and then withholds the conclusion: "Maybe they're onto something." (same). The hedge is deliberate: he reports evidence, not certainty.

**The agency ad: diagnosed as a formatting failure.** "Most agency marketing looks something like this." Then three lines of strategy for Search Intelligence: "Do great work", "Share how you did it", "Run as ad on founder's personal account" (Feb 20 2024). The critique of the default is that it is abstract and institutional; the fix is to publish the work and put a human's name and face on it. His summary of the difference is a single image: "Straight up steak on a plate." (same).

**AG1: diagnosed as scope creep.** "There's ONE way to buy Athletic Greens." Then the counterfactual: "Imagine AG1 mango. Or strawberry kiwi. Different sizes. Stick packs. 'Let's make a bar'. Now it's just another nutrition company." (Feb 20 2024). The fix is subtraction. He names the mechanism with Trout and Ries ("The power of your brand is inversely proportional to its scope.") and the behavioural mechanism with a phrase borrowed from web design: "Don't make me think." (same).

**The seven car sentences: a critique of one's own drafts.** The seven sentences are images in the original send and are missing from the text export, but the diagnosis is explicit: five of seven say nothing, and the writer cannot fix them until they can see them. "To stop writing them, you've got to spot them." (Feb 27 2024). The recommended drill is discrimination training on other people's sentences, not more writing.

**The intro he rewrites in public.** "I always get a little nervous writing the intro." Then the standard he is failing: "I want it to feel like I just wrote it. Fresh bread." Then the admission that there is no method: "There's no formula for 'fresh'. So I hack around 'til I read something back I like." (Feb 20 2024). This is a critique of polish itself: the goal is the feeling of immediacy, and the only way to test for it is to reread until something lands.

**The cold email he did not answer.** Of 97 cold emails in a month, he replied to one. The diagnosis is not about subject lines or length, it is about the ask: the one he answered "didn't ask me to" (Jul 24 2024). The fix is subtraction of the request, and the supporting number is Lizzy's 5% to 25%. This is the corpus's clearest example of a critique that produces a single editable variable.

**The label critique.** "Don't write 'labels'". "Give me something tangible." (Jul 24 2024). He offers no example, so the reader has to supply one, which is probably why the rule sticks. In practice the fix is to replace every classifying noun with a physical detail: not an adjective about leather but the thickness, the smell, the stitch count.

## Contrarian positions

**Against the AI shortcut.** "There is no AI prompt for conviction." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). He is not arguing that the tools do not work; he is arguing that the thing they cannot supply is the thing that makes work good. The surrounding passage is an inventory of cheap abundance: "Here's an unlimited amount of words. Here's 100 Canva templates." (same). The conclusion is that abundance is the problem.

**Against comfort and tooling.** "I feel safe. No blank page, no writer's block. The tools comfort me." Then the reversal: "No, they are the life preserver that sinks you." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). This is a direct attack on the productivity orthodoxy that removes friction. He wants the friction: "You are supposed to feel afraid." (same).

**Against the goal-setting platitude.** "If you ask Google how to write with purpose, you will be told platitudes like 'define your goal'." (One hundred days, May 01 2025). His alternative is less comfortable: "Be provocative." (same). The contrarian move is to reject the planning frame entirely and replace it with a provocation frame.

**Against testing as a substitute for having an idea.** "A tagline isn't a big idea. A trend isn't a big idea. A moodboard isn't a big idea. A list of adjectives isn't a big idea." Then: "Dancing isn't a big idea. Let's A/B test it isn't a big idea." (I'm back, Apr 01 2025). Testing is grouped with dancing and moodboards as a thing people do when they have nothing to say.

**Against the broad list.** The Dream 100 story is an argument against scale in prospecting: 2,200 names reduced to 167, and the 167 produce the Xerox deal. "So Chet ditches the list and goes all-in on the 167." (Feb 27 2024). Most outbound advice says volume; he says concentration.

**Against asking.** "Don't ask, don't get!" (Jul 24 2024), written immediately after a story in which not asking tripled the response rate. The proverb is deliberately inverted. The conventional sales instinct, to push for the call, is exactly what he says kills the reply.

**Against options.** "But options are harmless, right? You'd rather a choice." (Feb 20 2024). The sentence is sarcastic, and the next line is the law that refutes it. The prevailing ecommerce doctrine is to add variants; he says every variant dilutes the brand until it is "just another nutrition company" (same).

**Against starting with adjectives.** "Adjectives are okay. Just *ideas* don't start with them." (Jul 24 2024). Most briefs begin with three adjectives. He says the adjectives are the thing you arrive at, not the thing you depart from.

**Against judging work on a canvas.** "Get off Photoshop. It never looks like the pdf." (Feb 20 2024). The industry habit is to review a comp in isolation and approve it. He says the only valid test is comparative and in-context: "Stick it up against others. Which one do you read?" (same).

**Against promotional newsletters.** "I work hard to keep it short, it's never promotional" (Apr 19 2024). Said in an email whose purpose is to make people stay subscribed. The contrarian part is that he refuses to sell the subscription while asking for the subscription.

**Against the idea that the idea is the work.** The whole of "I'm back" argues that conviction and execution are the scarce inputs, not ideation. "And then you micromanage every word, every line, every take, every edit." (Apr 01 2025). The creative-director model, where the idea is handed off, is what this sentence rejects.
