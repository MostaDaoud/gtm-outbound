# The Value Equation — Offer Scoring & Rewriting

Load when scoring or rewriting an offer in `gtm-outbound-offer`.

## The Equation

```
                Dream Outcome  ×  Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
Value  =  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
              Perceived Time Delay  ×  Perceived Effort & Sacrifice
```

Two levers to maximize, two to minimize. The framing is Alex Hormozi's.

**"Perceived" governs three of the four drivers, not just likelihood.** It is easy to
read the bottom of the equation as calendar time and actual work; the source does not,
and neither should the scoring. A six-month engagement that shows something real in week
one is *perceived* as fast. An ask whose scope the reader cannot picture is *perceived*
as heavy even when the real work is an hour. You are scoring the offer as a stranger
reads it before they trust you, which is the only vantage point that predicts a reply.

**Drive the bottom toward zero before inflating the top.** The instinct is to make the
dream outcome bigger, because claims are free — which is exactly why they carry no
weight from a stranger. Time and effort are where a cold offer is actually won.

## Scoring Rubric

Score 1-5 on each. Score cold — as a stranger reading one email, not as someone who has
seen your deck.

### Dream Outcome (maximize)

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | An outcome they have never articulated wanting |
| 2 | Nice to have; would not fund it this year |
| 3 | Real want, but not urgent |
| 4 | Actively working on this problem right now |
| 5 | On a board deck or someone's performance review |

Most offers score higher here than they deserve, because founders confuse "the outcome
is valuable" with "they already want it." If nobody has budgeted for it, it is a 3.

### Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (maximize)

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | No proof of any kind |
| 2 | Generic claims, no numbers |
| 3 | Case studies from a different industry or company size |
| 4 | Named results from recognizably similar companies |
| 5 | Named results from direct peers, with numbers and timeframes |

**This is the lever that fails most often in B2B**, and it is the one cold outbound is
worst at carrying, because a stranger's claim about themselves has near-zero weight.
Specificity is the fix — "cut CAC 34% for three Clutch-listed agencies in 90 days" does
work that "we help agencies grow" cannot.

### Perceived Time Delay (minimize — score 5 = shortest)

Score the wait to the **first thing they can see**, not to full delivery. That is the
number they are estimating while they read.

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | 12+ months before anything visible |
| 2 | 6-12 months before anything visible |
| 3 | 3-6 months before anything visible |
| 4 | Weeks to a first signal |
| 5 | Days, or a diagnostic result before they commit |

The strongest move here is inventing an early signal — an audit, a teardown, a first
data pull — that is real and arrives fast, rather than compressing the actual delivery.
This is why the lever is *perceived*: the delivery timeline can be unchanged and the
score still moves.

### Perceived Effort & Sacrifice (minimize — score 5 = least)

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | Replace a system, retrain a team, migrate data |
| 2 | Significant internal project, multiple stakeholders |
| 3 | Sustained involvement from one team |
| 4 | A few hours of one person's time |
| 5 | Done for them; they approve and get out of the way |

Include political cost, not just labor. "Who has to admit the last vendor failed" is
effort, and it is often the real blocker.

Score what they **believe** is being asked. An ask whose scope a stranger cannot picture
is estimated at the high end by default, so vagueness is itself a score-1 answer — the
fix is naming the first step precisely, not promising that it will be easy.

## Diagnosing the Weakest Lever

Total score is close to meaningless. **The lowest single score is the campaign.**

| Weakest lever | What the campaign must do |
|---|---|
| Dream Outcome | Wrong segment. Go back to sub-niche selection — do not fix this with copy. |
| Perceived Likelihood | Lead with proof and specificity. Narrow to the niche where your proof is strongest. |
| Perceived Time Delay | Invent and lead with a fast first signal. |
| Perceived Effort & Sacrifice | Name the first step precisely, or remove work from the offer. |

A tie between two lowest levers usually means the segment is wrong, not that the offer
needs two fixes.

## The Rewrite Pattern

Once the weak lever is identified, rewrite so the offer attacks it directly.

**Before** (likelihood = 2):
> We help agencies improve their client acquisition through better data.

Nothing here is checkable. "Agencies" is not a segment, "improve" is not a number,
"better data" is not a mechanism.

**After** (likelihood = 4):
> We help Clutch-listed performance agencies with 15-60 staff cut cost per booked call
> by 30-40% within 60 days, by rebuilding their outbound data pipeline so reps stop
> working stale lists.

Named segment, named metric, named timeframe, named mechanism. Every clause is something
a prospect can check or dispute — which is what makes it credible rather than confident.

## Scoring the Rewrite

Re-score after rewriting and record both. The delta is the argument for the new
positioning, and it makes the reasoning auditable later when the campaign underperforms
and someone asks why this segment.

If the rewrite does not move the weak lever by at least 2 points, it was a wording
change, not a repositioning. Try again.

## UVP Formulation

Steve Blank's form, applied after scoring:

> We help **[sub-niche]** achieve **[measurable outcome]** by **[mechanism]**.

Rules:

- `[sub-niche]` must match the filter set exactly. If you cannot filter for it, it is
  not a niche.
- `[measurable outcome]` needs a number and a timeframe.
- `[mechanism]` explains *why it works*, and is what separates you from three
  competitors making the same outcome claim.

Write two or three variants targeting different pains in the same sub-niche. These
become the A/B arms downstream, so they must differ on substance — a different pain, a
different mechanism — not on phrasing.
