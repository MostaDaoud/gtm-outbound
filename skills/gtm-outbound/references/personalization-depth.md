# Personalization Depth — Evidence, Inference, and Boundaries

Load when writing an observation line, evaluating whether personalization is real, or
deciding whether a detail is appropriate to use.

`copy-frameworks.md` establishes that relevance beats shallow personalization. This file
covers the harder question: when personalization *is* worth doing, how do you do it
without either faking insight or being creepy.

## Personalization Is Graded, Not Binary

Teams call everything "personalized," which makes the word useless. Six levels:

| Level | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **0 — Merge field** | Inserts an identifier, changes no argument | "Hi Maya, saw you're VP Sales at Acme." |
| **1 — Observational** | Repeats a public fact | "Saw Acme is hiring enterprise AEs in Saudi." |
| **2 — Contextual** | Ties the fact to a role or market pattern | "That usually accompanies a move upmarket." |
| **3 — Interpretive** | Says what it means operationally | "That hiring pattern suggests regional coverage became a board priority." |
| **4 — Hypothesis-linked** | Connects it to a plausible problem | "The pressure point is likely proof that works with local enterprise buyers, not lead volume." |
| **5 — Perspective-led** | Offers a reading they may not have | "Expansion teams localise campaign copy first. The urgent gap is usually sales evidence." |

**Level 1 is where most "personalized" outbound stops.** It proves you opened LinkedIn. It
demonstrates no judgment, and the recipient can tell.

Depth is not length. A 60-word message can reach level 5. A 300-word one can sit at level 1.

**Match level to account value.** Level 4–5 costs real research time and only pays on
accounts worth it. For volume segments, relevance at level 2 plus a strong offer beats a
thin attempt at level 4 — and see the time-boxing rule below before committing.

## Observation, Inference, Hypothesis

The discipline that separates insight from confident nonsense. Three distinct things,
routinely collapsed into one:

| | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | A verifiable fact, with a source and a date | "Acme posted five Saudi enterprise AE roles on 12 March." |
| **Inference** | A reasonable reading of it | "Saudi looks like it is becoming a scaled motion, not coverage." |
| **Hypothesis** | A problem or opportunity that may follow | "New sellers may lack regional proof for procurement." |

Strong outreach moves through all three in one or two sentences. Weak outreach states the
observation and stops (level 1), or states the hypothesis as fact (which is worse — it
claims knowledge you do not have, and a recipient who knows better stops reading).

**Before writing, record it explicitly:**

```
OBSERVATION      [fact] — [source], [date]
INFERENCE        This may indicate [reading]
HYPOTHESIS       A likely consequence is [problem]
CONFIDENCE       high / medium / low, because [reason]
ALTERNATIVE      It could also just be [innocent explanation]
ROLE RELEVANCE   For a [role] this matters because [accountability]
```

The **alternative** line does the most work. "They are hiring salespeople" does not prove
a messaging problem — they might simply be growing. Forcing yourself to name the innocent
explanation is what keeps the message a hypothesis rather than an accusation.

**Language that signals a hypothesis honestly:**

> "It looks like…" · "I may be wrong, but…" · "Teams at that stage often…" ·
> "I'd expect…" · "One possibility is…" · "Curious whether you're seeing…"

Use **one** of these per message. Hedging every sentence reads as evasive and undermines
the point of having a view at all.

## Common Ground That Earns Its Place

Shared attributes are not automatically persuasive. Similarity works when it makes your
*interpretation* more credible — not when it is merely true.

**Weak:** same university, same city, same nationality, same football team, same former
employer. These are conversational texture at best. Used as an opener, they read as bait —
and worse when the pivot to business is abrupt.

**Four bridges that carry weight:**

| Bridge | Connects through | Weak → Strong |
|---|---|---|
| **Experience** | A situation you have both been in | "We both worked at startups" → "We've both been in teams where sales rewrote the deck before every meeting" |
| **Belief** | A genuine shared view of how something works | "Great post" → "Your split between translation and buyer proof matches what I see in GCC launches" |
| **Constraint** | A limitation you both understand | "Budgets are tight" → "Building regional motion before a local marketing team forces one of two bad choices" |
| **Pattern** | A cause-and-effect you have seen repeatedly | "We work with companies like yours" → "Across three enterprise transitions, the first break was late-stage deals stalling on proof built for smaller buyers" |

**The pattern bridge is usually the strongest for outbound.** It demonstrates expertise
through an observed causal relationship rather than by claiming expertise. You never have
to write "we are experts" — the observation does it.

The best common ground is rarely "we are alike." It is **"we have both seen this
problem."**

Only use a personal commonality when it is genuine, easy to explain how you know it, and
contributes to the business conversation. If the only reason to include a detail is to
prove you did research, cut it.

## The Boundary — When Personalization Becomes Creepy

The skill has strong rules about *accuracy*. This is about *appropriateness*, which is a
separate failure and a worse one — it does not just fail to convert, it damages the sender.

**A working rule:**

> The recipient should immediately understand why the information was publicly available
> and why it is relevant to this conversation.

If either half fails, do not use it.

**Do not use:**

- Personal but professionally irrelevant details
- Anything where they would have to wonder how you found it
- Family, health, religion, politics, or other sensitive traits
- Precise location or movement
- Stale personal social activity
- Anything obviously scraped at population scale

| Creepy | Relevant |
|---|---|
| "Saw from Instagram you were in Alexandria last weekend." | "Your interview about building from Egypt for regional buyers stood out." |
| "Noticed your daughter just started at [school]." | "You mentioned hiring is your constraint this quarter." |

**Public availability is not permission.** A detail being findable says nothing about
whether referencing it is appropriate. This applies with extra force where GDPR or similar
regimes govern the recipient — see `deliverability.md` for the jurisdiction table.

## The Relevance Chain

Build this before writing. Every link should be defensible.

```
signal → implication → problem → why you → offer
```

| Link | Question |
|---|---|
| **Signal** | What changed, and how do I know? |
| **Implication** | What does that probably mean operationally? |
| **Problem** | What usually becomes hard at that point? |
| **Why you** | What have I actually seen that makes this credible? |
| **Offer** | What can I give before asking for anything? |

If any link is missing, the message will feel like a leap — and the reader will locate the
gap faster than you did.

## Time-Boxing the Research

Depth has a cost, and unbounded research is how prospecting quietly stops happening.
A workable discipline: **five minutes of research, five facts collected, five minutes
writing.**

Search in priority order, because the early sources carry the most signal:

1. **Job postings, product and integration pages, developer docs** — live operational
   priorities, stated in their own words
2. **Tech-stack detection, executive posts, recent announcements** — account context
3. **Podcasts, conference talks, trade commentary** — first-person language and vocabulary

Stop at five facts. Bank the unused ones for follow-ups rather than cramming them in.

**Track cost, not just outcome.** The honest measure of whether deep personalization is
worth it is pipeline generated per research hour — not reply rate alone. A level-5 message
that takes 40 minutes and doubles reply rate may still lose to a level-2 message that
takes four. Measure it per segment rather than assuming.

## The Test Before Sending

Strip the name, company, and title. Read it again.

**Could this go to a hundred other people?** If yes, the personalization is cosmetic
regardless of how many facts it contains.

Then the second test, from `copy-frameworks.md`: remove the personalized opening entirely.
Does the message still make sense? If it does, the personalization was decoration — it was
never carrying weight.
